Résumé
Le développement logiciel est confronté à des défis persistants en matière de maintenabilité et d’efficacité, ce
qui motive la recherche continue d’approches innovantes. Les méthodologies Agiles, notamment le Behaviour-
Driven Development (BDD), ont pris de l’ampleur dans la société grâce à leur capacité à favoriser la réactivité
face aux changements et la communication entre les parties prenantes. Cependant, comme pour beaucoup de
méthodes, le BDD peut entraîner des coûts de maintenance et des problèmes de productivité. Pour relever
ces défis, cette recherche étudie l’adaptation de techniques avancées de génération automatique de données, en
particulier SELF-INSTRUCT, pour augmenter les ensembles de données BDD.
    L’étude commence par analyser l’état de l’art des méthodologies Agile, du traitement du langage naturel
(NLP) et des outils d’automatisation, en soulignant les limites des approches existantes et le potentiel de la
méthode SELF-INSTRUCT. Ensuite, la section méthodologie détaille l’adaptation de la méthode au contexte
BDD et la création d’ensembles de données dont la qualité des données initiales diffère. Les résultats indiquent
que SELF-INSTRUCT augmente efficacement les ensembles de données BDD, doublant la taille de l’ensemble
d’origine et produisant des scénarios utilisables. Cependant, des améliorations sont nécessaires, notamment
dans la phase de filtrage, pour améliorer la qualité des données générées et acceptées par la méthode.
    De plus, l’étude révèle que la qualité des données initiales influence la validité des scénarios générés, un
ensemble initial de bonne qualité produit un plus grand nombre de paires valides. Néanmoins, tous les ensembles
étudiés aboutissent à des scénarios utilisables, cela indique l’efficacité de SELF-INSTRUCT quelle que soit la
qualité des données initialement fournies. La recherche suggère des pistes d’exploration future, particulièrement
un perfectionnement ultérieur de la méthode et l’étude de différents modèles de langage.
    En conclusion, cette recherche confirme le potentiel des techniques de génération automatique de données,
telles que SELF-INSTRUCT, pour augmenter efficacement et substantiellement la taille des ensembles com-
posés de données du domaine BDD. Malgré ses limites, cette étude jette les bases de recherches futures visant
à améliorer l’augmentation des ensembles de données BDD et à faire progresser le domaine de la génération
automatisée de données en génie logiciel.

Abstract
Software development faces persistent challenges in terms of maintainability and efficiency, and this is driving
the ongoing search for innovative approaches. Agile methodologies, in particular Behaviour-Driven Develop-
ment (BDD), have gained ground in society thanks to their ability to promote responsiveness to change and
communication between stakeholders. However, as with many methods, the use of BDD can lead to mainte-
nance costs and productivity problems. To meet these challenges, this research investigates the adaptation of
advanced automatic data generation techniques, in particular SELF-INSTRUCT, to augment BDD datasets.
    The research starts by analysing the state of the art in Agile methodologies, natural language processing
(NLP) and automation tools, highlighting the limitations of existing approaches and the potential of the SELF-
INSTRUCT method. Next, the methodology section details the adaptation of the method to the BDD context
and the creation of datasets with different initial data quality. The results indicate that SELF-INSTRUCT
effectively augments BDD datasets, doubling the size of the original dataset and producing usable scenarios.
However, improvements are needed, particularly in the filtering phase, to improve the quality of the data
generated and accepted by the method.
    In addition, the study reveals that the quality of the initial data influences the validity of the scenarios
generated, with good quality seed producing a greater number of valid pairs. Nevertheless, all the sets studied
resulted in usable scenarios, indicating the effectiveness of SELF-INSTRUCT regardless of seed quality. The
research suggests ideas for future exploration, including further fine-tuning of the method and investigation of
different language models.
    In conclusion, this research confirms the potential of automated data generation techniques, such as SELF-
INSTRUCT, to efficiently and substantially increase the size of sets composed of BDD data. Despite its
limitations, this study lays the foundation for future research aimed at improving BDD dataset augmentation
and advancing the field of automated data generation in software engineering.

Keywords : Software engineering, Agile methodology, Behaviour-Driven Development, SELF-INSTRUCT,
Natural Language Processing, Language Models, Data Generation, Dataset Augmentation

Chapter 1

Introduction

In recent years, Agile methods have become increasingly established in the world of software development [23].
This type of method emphasizes iterative development, collaboration and rapid adaptation to change. In-
deed, change has become omnipresent and increasingly rapid in today’s society. These Agile methods include
Behaviour-Driven Development (BDD) [28], which is a variant of Test-Driven Development (TDD) [3] in which
test cases are based on user story scenarios. The BDD method has proved to be a powerful approach for aligning
software development with business objectives. It places particular emphasis on the use of natural language
within the scenarios used for testing, in order to facilitate communication between the development team and
stakeholders. However, the adoption of the BDD method can lead to significant maintenance costs that can
reduce team productivity [7]. Maintenance costs are a very common problem in the IT sector. To solve this
problem in the context of BDD, the researchers explored the possibility of automatically deriving test cases from
user story scenarios [37]. Nevertheless, their approach is naive, and these automatic generation tools are often
unable to produce scenarios that meet certain essential quality criteria [29], which leads to inefficiencies in the
testing process [9]. Challenges then remain in using these tools to create high-quality scenarios that accurately
capture system behaviour.
        Given recent advances in Natural Language Processing (NLP) and the rapid progress of Large Language
Models (LLMs), such as GPT-3, GPT-4, Alpaca, etc., it is now possible for these models to generate code with
impressive accuracy and complexity. This raises the question whether an optimised version of these models
could be used to generate high-quality BDD data more efficiently [8]. By “optimised version” we mean that the
automatic generation tools should be adapted to work with a different type of data. Thus, in order to fine-
tune such models for BDD data generation, a complete dataset of user story scenarios and their corresponding
implementations should be built as a reference. However, it is important to note that examples available on
platforms such as GitHub and other online sources can often be of poor-quality or incomplete [29]. In such
cases, it becomes necessary to create a whole new set of examples from scratch to meet the limitations of the
available online resources. This highlights the need for robust, high-quality datasets to effectively train and
fine-tune BDD data generation models.
        In general, creating a new dataset from scratch is a resource-intensive and time-consuming process. For
this reason, many methods choose to use existing online data as it is provided. However, as mentioned above,
the quality and completeness of this data can vary considerably. Recently, a method called SELF-INSTRUCT
has emerged as a promising approach to augmenting datasets [43]. This method has demonstrated the ability
to create large datasets from a few initial human-written instances. It exploits the capabilities of large language
models such as GPT-3 to generate synthetic data [33]. The aim of this research is to determine whether
SELF-INSTRUCT can be adapted to generate a large dataset of user story scenarios and their corresponding
implementations to facilitate development via the BDD method. In the next chapter, we take a closer look at the
current landscape of Agile methodologies, the NLP domain and automation tools, and identify the limitations of
existing approaches in this context. By understanding these limitations, it will be easier to explore the potential
of SELF-INSTRUCT to augment BDD datasets.
        Subsequently, this master thesis takes a closer look at the adaptation of the SELF-INSTRUCT method to
the BDD domain for the purpose of augmenting a set of scenarios with their implementations. In this experiment,
scenarios will be written in Gherkin, a language used in BDD, and their corresponding implementations will be
written in Python following the “behave” testing framework. The methodology describes the specific adaptations
made to the method, including the selection of a large language model (LLM) to replace GPT-3 which was
initially used in the SELF-INSTRUCT method. The reasons behind these changes are discussed, considering
factors such as model performance and availability. Additionally, the process of creating the original set used by
the method is described in detail. This original set serves as the base for the procedure, allowing it to generate
new instances effectively.

        In the rest of the research, the generated data by SELF-INSTRUCT are collected for study and analysis.

is an opportunity to fine-tune and extend the capabilities of the tested method. This could involve exploring

BDD testing.
        In summary, SELF-INSTRUCT represents a breakthrough in BDD test automation, offering a scalable
approach to scenario generation. By exploiting the power of artificial intelligence and natural language process-
ing, it is possible to hope to meet the challenges of maintaining test sets while keeping pace with the dynamic
nature of software development.

2.1     Software engineering
Today, “software is deeply embedded in virtually every aspect of our lives” [31], so the software development
industry continues to grow, and with it the need for high-quality software. Software development is also known
as software engineering. Although some make a distinction between these two terms, considering the first to
be a specific phase of the latter, which is the overall process described below. This one refers to the process of
creating (communication and planning), designing (modelling), programming (building), testing, deploying and
maintaining software and applications [31]. Quality software is software that aims to provide an optimal user
experience, in other words, an experience that respects:
   • Functional suitability: Able to provide the functionality required to meet the specified needs.
   • Performance efficiency
   • Compatibility: Compatible with various media.
   • Usability: User-friendly.
   • Reliability
   • Security
   • Maintainability
   • Portability
while minimising the problems, risks, and costs associated with its use and maintenance [15]. It is the result
of careful design, rigorous testing and continuous quality management throughout its life cycle. A “life cycle”
is the set of distinct and sequential phases through which software passes throughout its existence, from the
initial design phase to the end of its useful life. Each phase of the cycle is associated with specific activities,
deliverables, and objectives [12]. There are various software life cycle models, the most commonly used of
which include the Waterfall model (see Figure 2.1a), the V-model (see Figure 2.1b), and agile development
methodologies (see Figure 2.2). It is these latter methodologies that will be the focus of this work.

2.1.1    Agile methodologies
Agile methodologies are project management approaches based on flexibility and adaptability, aimed at re-
sponding effectively and quickly to changes and evolving needs. They encourage ongoing collaboration between
members of the development team and stakeholders (individuals, groups, or entities that are directly or indi-
rectly affected by the project), encouraging frequent and transparent communication. These stakeholders may
influence or be influenced by the project’s decisions, activities or results, as well as having specific interests,
expectations, or concerns related to the project. In addition to communication, most agile based approaches
rely on iterations (also called “sprint”) during which product functionalities are developed, tested and delivered.
Each of these iterations is generally of short duration, providing the flexibility that characterises this type of
method. The main objective is to deliver concrete results at regular intervals, enabling the project direction
to be adjusted quickly in response to feedback on the current product and any new requirements that may
arise [3]. In short, Agile methods “offer an effective solution to the challenges currently facing the software
industry. [...] The increasing adoption of Agile methods in the software industry proves their usefulness and
effectiveness” [23]. These methods focus on customer satisfaction, product quality and proactive collaboration.
They encourage team self-organisation, collective decision-making and continuous adaptability to change. The
twelve Agile principles are listed in the Agile Manifesto, which emphasises the value of individuals and interac-
tions, operational software, collaboration with the customer, and responsiveness to change [5].
The twelve principles are [5]:
  1. The main priority is to satisfy the customer by delivering solutions that add value quickly and consistently.
  2. You have to remain flexible in the face of changing needs, even late in the process. Changes are an
     opportunity to improve the customer’s competitive edge.
  3. Operational solutions need to be delivered frequently, with a preference for shorter lead times.
  4. The people in the business and the implementation teams work together every day throughout the project.
  5. You need to build projects with motivated people, and provide them the environment and support they
     need to succeed.
  6. Face-to-face conversations are the most effective and least expensive way of communicating information
     within the team.
  7. The main measure of project progress is the number of operational solutions available.
  8. A sustainable pace must be maintained throughout the project for the sponsors, the developers, and the
     users.
  9. Constant attention must be paid to technical compliance and quality of design to enhance agility.
 10. Simplicity is essential.
 11. The best architectures, specifications, and designs come from self-organised teams.
 12. We need to reflect on our effectiveness at regular intervals and adjust the team’s behaviour accordingly.
Despite the many qualities of agile methodologies, many developers may encounter problems when implementing
certain practices such as Test-Driven Development (TDD). TDD is a method based on building automated tests
before the source code is even written [3]. The process therefore begins with the creation of a unit test that
specifies a functionality or expected behaviour of the system. Unit testing is the practice of individually testing
the most elementary components of software, such as functions and methods in the source code, independently
of the rest of the code, to ensure that each unit functions correctly. The developer then writes the code needed
to pass the test while respecting the two main rules of TDD [4]:
2.1. SOFTWARE ENGINEERING                                                   CHAPTER 2. STATE OF THE ART


  1. If the test fails, you need to write code to resolve it.

  2. Do not duplicate code.

Note that the software still needs to be tested using other types of test. The iteration between writing tests
and writing code is continuous, with tests being run frequently to ensure that all existing functionality remains
operational [4]. Moreover, TDD promotes modular design, code clarity and early error detection, as each new
feature is validated by automated tests [22]. Test automation aims to reduce the need for repetitive manual
intervention when validating and verifying software functionality. It is used to improve the efficiency, quality,
and speed of the testing process [3]. The problem with this approach is that it is not always easy to identify
what needs to be tested. Moreover, certain aspects of the code can also be difficult to test in this way, such
as interactions between different parts of the system, components that depend on external environments, or
aspects relating to the user interface that are more appropriately the subject of other types of verification.
Developers may also find it difficult to define exhaustive test cases to cover all possible situations. Whatever
the case, these problems can be (partially) resolved by using other methods.

2.1.2    Behaviour-Driven Development
A behavioural approach can help to manage the problems of methods such as TDD. Behaviour-Driven Devel-
opment (BDD) helps you to know where to start, what to test, how many items to test at a time, how to
name the tests and why a test fails [28]. This approach, directly derived from TDD, encourages the writing of
tests in the form of sentences describing what is expected of the software. In other ways, this approach allows
clearer documentation and facilitates communication between development teams, analysts, and business users
(people who use the system developed to carry out tasks related to their professional responsibilities). On the
other hand, it can also lead to better software design, for example, by encouraging the creation of distinct
classes for distinct responsibilities [28]. In computer science, a class is a fundamental structure, a model, for
creating objects, which are instances (an instance is a specific example) of this class. Objects are entities that
group together data (also called attributes) and methods (also called functions or procedures) that operate on
this data. In addition to these assets, the BDD uses a precise structure comprising features, scenarios and
their implementations. A feature encapsulates a set of BDD scenarios that describe different aspects of the
same functionality; this feature is used to create a user story [36]. A user story is represented by the following
template:
[StoryTitle] (Describe the user story in one line)
As a [Role]
I want a [Feature]
So that I can get [Benefit]

        The title of the user story describes an activity carried out by a user in a given role. The feature provided
by the system enables the user to perform the activity in order to obtain a benefit [28]. By following this model,
anybody (developers or other stakeholders of the project) can clearly understand what functionality the system
needs to support and why.
        Developers then know what behaviour they need to implement and with whom to analyse and discuss the
functionality. Moreover, because of this, users should be able to describe the benefits they will get from using the
feature to justify its existence [36]. A language commonly used to represent features and scenarios is Gherkin.
Gherkin is a specification language designed to describe the expected behaviour of software in a way that is
readable and understandable by developers and non-technical stakeholders [35]. A specification refers to a formal
or informal description of requirements and desired behaviour. They are usually drawn up before (and during, in
the case of agile methods) development and describe how the software should work, what capabilities it should
offer, how it should interact with other systems, and often include technical details about architecture and
implementation. There are different levels, ranging from specifications of general requirements (functional, the
software’s functionality, and non-functional, security, reliability, etc.) to specifications of software architecture
and implementation (performance, location, acceptability, etc. [24]) to detailed system specifications. They can
take different forms when used in other types of methodologies, including text documents, diagrams, models or
formal languages, depending on the needs of the project. In the context of BDD and Gherkin, they appear in
the form of scenarios. They are essential to ensure a common understanding of a project and to guarantee that
the end product meets expectations. A scenario, therefore, being a specification representation, is a detailed
and structured description of a precise behaviour expected of a system and written in a natural language that
everyone can understand. The main objective of BDD scenarios is to describe the desired behaviour of a
function or part of the system from the user’s point of view. They follow a specific structure to describe the
initial conditions, actions and expected results of a scenario [35].

The Gherkin specification format is mainly based on specific keywords to define scenes and steps. Basic keywords
include [35]:

   • Feature: Defines a software feature.

   • Scenario: Defines a specific usage scenario.

   • Given: Defines the initial state or preconditions.

   • When: Defines the action or trigger.

   • Then: Defines the expected result, the post-condition.

Here is a simple example of a Gherkin specification [35]:
Feature: Authentication user
As a user
I want to login
So that I can acces the website

  Scenario: Successful login
    Given the user has a valid account
    When the user logs in with their username and password
    Then the user should be redirected to the home page

  Scenario: Login failure with incorrect information
    Given the user has a valid account
    When the user logs in with incorrect information
    Then the user should see an error message

It is also possible to add other keywords [35]:

   • Background: Describes a common context that applies to several scenarios, thus simplifying writing by
     avoiding the repetition of similar steps. This makes BDD specifications easier to read and maintain, as
     changes to the common context only require a single modification, rather than one per scenario.

   • And: Adds additional steps after the initial “Given”, “When”, or “Then” keywords to extend the scenario.

   • But: Defines the desired result or objective to be achieved at the end of the execution of a scenario. It is
     used to clarify expectations after a “Then”.

This structure makes it easier to read and describe behaviours and ensures consistency in the use of the BDD.

       As well as representing the specifications, each scenario corresponds to a step function. These functions
implement each of the steps in a scenario in order to verify and validate the system’s behaviour. Gherkin is often
used with automated testing tools, such as Cucumber (an open source testing framework) [35], which interprets
Gherkin specifications and executes them, automating the tests. It is now clear that the BDD method has
many advantages. However, it involves maintenance problems and therefore affects the overall quality of the
software [7]. What’s more, when there are numerous examples and scenarios, the specifications can become
costly to maintain and extend. As a result, some projects find it difficult to modify the examples associated
with parts of the system, which can lead to stagnation of these parts. There are other challenges such as
detecting and managing duplication in specifications, locating defects in large BDD sets and making them
faster, and making changes to existing specifications [7]. All these elements make it possible to identify several
research opportunities, in particular the need to undertake investigations into duplication detection techniques,
to facilitate the understanding of specifications and to improve the maintenance of BDD tests. The latter is the
main focus of this research.


2.2     Natural Language Processing
Natural Language Processing (NLP) is a field of artificial intelligence (AI) whose aim is to enable machines
to understand, interpret and generate natural language to perform useful activities [25]. In recent years, NLP
has moved from representation learning and the design of architectures for a single task, to the use of task-
independent architectures. Representation learning, in this context, refers to training a model to extract relevant
2.2. NATURAL LANGUAGE PROCESSING                                         CHAPTER 2. STATE OF THE ART


features or structures from input data, so that these representations can be used for specific tasks. This change
has led to considerable progress on several challenging NLP tasks, such as reading comprehension or answering
questions [8]. There are a number of classic NLP methods in which word frequency is the key. In these, text
is transformed into a vector form containing the number of words appearing in a document. Categorical word
representation is the simplest way of representing text, in which words are represented by the symbols “1” or
“0”. Here are two classic Categorical word representation methods:

   • One hot encoding: The simplest method of representing text. In this encoding, the dimension corre-
     sponds to the number of terms in the vocabulary. Each term is expressed as a binary variable (0 or 1),
     which means that each word is made up solely of zeros and ones. The index of the word is marked with
     1, while all the others are marked with 0s. Each word has a unique dimension and will be represented by
     a 1 in that dimension, with 0s everywhere else [25].

   • Bag-of-Words (BoW): BoW is an extension of One hot encoding. It adds up the one-hot encoding of
     the words present in the sentence. As the vocabulary grows, so does the length of the vectors. Too many
     “0s” can produce a sparse matrix containing no information about the semantics, word order or grammar
     used in the sentences [25].

one-hot encoding and “Hello World” for BoW [25] :

       These methods lead to a fundamental concept in NLP, the concept of embedding. Word embedding is a
technique in which text is mapped into vectors. In other words, it allows words with similar meaning (those used
and appearing in similar contexts) to have embedding in the same representation. Its most important advantage
is that it provides a more efficient and expressive representation by preserving the similarity of the context of
the words and using low-dimensional vectors [25]. The use of embedding techniques has proven useful in many
NLP tasks, as they capture the semantics of words as well as their syntactic information while maintaining word
order. That said, the popularity of word embedding methods stems from two models, Word2Vec and GloVe:

  1. Word2vec: This is a word representation model that uses two hidden layers in a neural network to create
     a vector representing each word. Note that a neural network refers to a software hardware architecture
     that mimics the behaviour and capabilities of real biological neural networks. The ambition of such
     a network is to build powerful computing tools to solve problems [44]. The word vectors captured by
     Word2vec’s Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) and Skip-gram models are supposed to contain semantic
     and syntactic information. Word2Vec was developed to make training of embedding more meaningful and
     has since been used as the standard method for developing a pre-trained word representation. Depending
     on the context, Word2Vec predicts using either Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW) or Skip-gram.

        • Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW): CBOW predicts words based on their context by commu-
          nicating with neighbouring words in the window. Three layers are used in the CBOW process. The
          first is the context, the second hidden layer corresponds to the estimation of each word from the
          input to the weight matrix, which is then estimated up to the third layer, the output. The last phase
          of this method consists of correlating the result and the work itself to improve the representation
          based on the backpropagation of the error gradient (see Figure 2.3). The CBOW method predicts
          the middle word according to its context, it is also faster and works better with repeated words than
          Skip-Gram [25].
        • Skip-Gram: This algorithm is the inverse of the previous model. The prediction is given on the
          basis of the central word after the formation of the context. The input layer correlates with the
          target word, and the output layer represents the context. This model seeks to estimate the context
          given the word. The last phase of this model is the correlation between the output and each word
          in the context to adjust the representation based on back-propagation (see Figure 2.3). Skip-gram is
          effective when there is less training data and infrequent words are well presented [25].

     Word2vec-trained word embedding makes it possible to better capture the semantics of words and ma-
     nipulate their relationships. However, Word2vec focuses mainly on local context, while global statistical
     information is not well-used.

2.2.1    Language models
As explained above, NLP is a very wide field that encompasses all the methods and techniques that enable
machines to understand and generate natural language. Language models, on the other hand, are specific NLP
concepts used to model the structure and relationships of language in order to achieve multiple objectives. More
specifically, a language model (LM) refers to a statistical or probabilistic representation of the appearance of
sequences of words in a given language. This type of model is used in the field of automatic NLP for various
tasks, such as speech recognition, machine translation, text generation, spelling correction and other language-
related applications [6]. A language model learns the structure and formality of the language, determined from
textual data. It can be based on simple statistics, such as word frequency (as the TF method does), or on more
2.2. NATURAL LANGUAGE PROCESSING                                         CHAPTER 2. STATE OF THE ART


advanced techniques using neural networks, such as language models based on transformers like OpenAI’s GPT
(Generative Pre-trained Transformer). GPT is one of the most famous families of large language models (LLMs).
LLMs are LMs that have particular properties related to their size. A language model can be considered “large”
when the number of parameters in its architecture is high (millions or billions). Size is important because it
allows the model to capture complex information, nuanced linguistic patterns, and perform more diverse tasks.
Thanks to size, LLMs are able to understand the subtle relationships between words, phrases, and even ideas.
They are tools that use AI to understand and generate natural language [6]. They are capable of processing
massive amounts of textual data and learning complex patterns. Many LLMs use transform architectures, which
are particularly well suited to processing sequences of data and understanding complex relationships, thanks to
the attention mechanism that allows the model to consider different parts of the sequence simultaneously. The
LLM GPT itself uses the Transformer decoder structure (illustrated on the right-hand side of Figure 2.4). This
architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. in 2017 [42], has played a crucial role in recent advances in NLP.
The pre-trained GPT model consists of 12 Transformer blocks [47].

it for specific tasks. These models have contributed significantly to advances in the NLP field.

2.2.2    Behave nicely
Improving maintenance can, in fact, be facilitated by automation, which has become essential in software
engineering. The term automation refers to the use of processes, software, or technologies to perform tasks
without direct human intervention. It aims to improve the efficiency, precision, speed, and reproducibility
of operations. In the case of BDD, the automatic management of test cases based on scenarios could
reduce the manual workload and the risk of human error when using the method [37]. In fact, this is
the objective of the Behave nicely approach, which automatically generates step functions from Gherkin
2.2. NATURAL LANGUAGE PROCESSING                                          CHAPTER 2. STATE OF THE ART


scenarios. This approach seeks to reduce the manual maintenance of these functions, which is often necessary
when specifications evolve (see Figure 2.8). Behave nicely uses NLP to extract information from Gherkin
scenarios available on GitHub, interpret it and generate step functions to verify and validate it. This ap-
proach focuses on the Python language for writing step implementation functions and Gherkin for the scenarios.

Behave nicely has three main components:

  1. The Change Detector: This first component detects when changes occur in the project and signals that
     the step functions need to be regenerated [37]. This is what makes it possible to monitor the evolution of
     the specifications.

  2. The Parsing component: This one performs part-of-speech tagging and surface semantic analysis of each
     instruction in Gherkin scenarios. It uses a combination of tagging implemented using NLTK (a popular
     library for NLP) [26], and semantic role labelling implemented using AllenNLP (a library focused on
     creating NLP models) [39, 37].

  3. The Generating component: This last component generates step function code using information from the
     parsing component and stores it in a steps.py file [37].




                                   Figure 2.8: Behave nicely architecture [37].

       This shows the essential role of NLP to Behave nicely. This approach has also been evaluated [37] using
the “mutation testing” technique, and the results suggest that there is significant potential for automating code
generation for BDD tests. Mutation testing is a software testing technique that aims to assess the quality of
a set of tests by deliberately introducing “mutations” (changes) into the program’s source code. The aim is to
measure the ability of existing tests to detect these mutations. If one of them is not detected by the tests, this
suggests that there is a weak point in the test suite. The advantage of mutation testing is its ability to identify
gaps in the test suite [11]. Although this type of testing can be resource-intensive and time-consuming, it
made sense for Behave nicely because the success of automatic test method generation depends on a number of
factors, including the conformity of test procedures in reference projects, naming conventions between Gherkin
specifications, and the explicitness of specifications in relation to the implementation of the corresponding test
function [37].
       In addition, it is important to mention that the examples of GitHub scenarios used by Behave nicely
can be both good and bad (incomplete or incorrect) examples. GitHub is a web platform based on Git, a
decentralised version management system. It offers a range of tools for version control, project management
and collaboration between members of a development team [14]. The web platform is widely used in the
software development industry to host projects and share source code (like the one used by the model presented
above). Although practical, not everything on GitHub is necessarily reliable [10]. This is in line with the
observations made with Behave nicely, which show that the quality of example scenarios has a strong influence
on the quality of those that emerge. This highlights the need to generate new data more reliably and efficiently.
The original scenarios must be of good quality to avoid the problems associated with incomplete or inconsistent
specifications. The problem with the quality of the scenarios comes partly from the lack of existing guidance
for the method. To compensate for the lack of existing guidance in this area, researchers have developed
a set of quality attributes and a question-based checklist to help assess the quality of BDD scenarios [29].

the quality of a scenario and to select a set of quality scenarios that would serve as the basis for a machine
learning method that could match or even surpass the results of tools such as Behave nicely.

2.2.3    SELF-INSTRUCT
Under these conditions, it could be suggested that the application of a self-learning method such as the one
mentioned above, to a possible set of quality scenarios, would be a promising approach. The method used by
Alpaca is called SELF-INSTRUCT [38]. It is a method that aims to improve the ability of pre-trained LLMs to
follow instructions using instructions generated by the model itself. The process generates instructions, inputs,
and outputs from a language model, then filters out invalid or similar instructions and uses them to fine-tune
the original model [43]. The application of SELF-INSTRUCT to the basic GPT-3 model automatically
generates a large-scale dataset (52,000 instructions) for various tasks and shows a clear improvement over
the original model on a task called SUPER-NATURALINSTRUCTIONS (1,616 NLP tasks and their natural
language instructions), comparable to the performance of InstructGPT (a model trained with private user
data and human annotations). A human evaluation also shows that the use of SELF-INSTRUCT to fine-tune
GPT-3 outperforms the use of existing public instruction sets and therefore outperforms the use of a set such
as the one used by Behave nicely (which comes solely from public data). It is therefore important to select a
quality set of instances in order to train the model.

In concrete terms, SELF-INSTRUCT is a four-stage process comprising [43] (see Figure 2.9):
  1. Instructions generation
  2. Identification of classification tasks
  3. Instances generation
  4. Filtering low-quality data
       However, the method, or at least its evaluation, has a number of limitations, including language model
biases, reliance on large models, and the possibility of amplifying undesirable social stereotypes [43].

3.1     Motivation
As explained in section 2.2.2, tools such as Behave nicely are based on datasets of varying quality depending on
the source of the data collected (GitHub, original data, etc.). For the moment, there is no dedicated method for
obtaining a large set of quality BDD data, nor is there a method for guaranteeing the quality of data collected
from online resources. In this context, it would be relevant to seek to create a large dataset where each can be
judged to be of good quality. Until now, the SELF-INSTRUCT method (see section 2.2.3), mentioned above,
has been used to augment instruction sets of various and varied natures. The authors of this method created a
set of 52,000 data sets from 175. In the context of BDD, it would be interesting to study the capabilities of this
model in the creation of scenario databases and their respective implementation of better quality (according to
the criteria of [29]). Ideally, as in the original use of SELF-INSTRUCT, it would be possible to create a very
large set of high-quality BDD data from a small set. The interest of this research is whether SELF-INSTRUCT
can be used to generate such a dataset. The research questions are:

 RQ1: To what extent is it possible to adapt Stanford Alpaca’s SELF-INSTRUCT method to generate a
    high-quality set of BDD data (Gherkin scenarios and their corresponding step functions implemented in
    Python)?

 RQ2: To what extent are the generated scenarios ill-formed?

 RQ3: To what extent does the SELF-INSTRUCT model used on examples of pairs of BDD scenarios and
    their implementations, said to be of good quality, produce results of higher quality than those of the same
    model on examples of variable quality?

The hypothesis is that, although it is possible to generate a relevant and efficient BDD scenario dataset, it is
not possible to guarantee the quality of each of the data sets generated via the SELF-INSTRUCT method. It
is also possible that the quality of the newly generated dataset does not depend on the quality of the training
data initially provided.


3.2     The methodology
This research is therefore based on the SELF-INSTRUCT method [43]. This method is divided into four main
stages, which exploit an original set of 175 data items created by humans and/or collected manually from various
sources. The methodology section can be divided into three subsections reporting:

  1. How the original method was adapted

  2. How the seed data set was created

  3. How the experiment was conduct

3.2.1    Adapting the original method
Before we start testing or creating the package itself, it is necessary to look at how the method works in order to
make it usable in this specific application context and adapt it to the hardware resources available. Originally,
SELF-INSTRUCT used OpenAI’s davinci model, but it was deprecated on 04 January 2024 and had to be
3.2. THE METHODOLOGY                                             CHAPTER 3. RESEARCH DEVELOPMENT




                                    Figure 3.1: Deprecations - OpenAI API


replaced. As there are so many LLMs to choose from, we first turned to the models directly recommended by
OpenAI in their deprecations: davinci-002, gpt-3.5-turbo or gpt-4 (see Figure 3.1).
       However, these are not Open Source and we prefer to use an equivalent Open Weight or Open Source
model. Therefore, the model that was chosen is a model recently published by Mistral AI, Mixtral-8x7B-
Instruct [16]. As seen in section 2.2.1, the performance of this model is close to that of gpt-3.5-turbo, it
seems suited to replacing the models from OpenAI. This model had also demonstrated that it can match the
performance of GPT-4 in some benchmarks while requiring less resources. In addition, the licence of this model
is Apache 2.0, which allows it to be used, modified and distributed freely. These features are really advantageous
because they encourage sharing and replicability, which are crucial in the research field. To use Mixtral-8x7B-
Instruct, we choose LangChain with Hugging Face endpoints on mistralai/Mixtral-8x7B-v0.1 because the model
is very large and very demanding in terms of resources so it is difficult to use it locally on a laptop. Moreover,
these tools proved to be the most suitable means of simplifying the use of language models and guaranteeing the
ability to change quickly and smoothly should another model be required. As soon as the replacement model
was selected, it was integrated into the project via the mixtral_api.py file, which is used by the different phases
of the method, and serves as a replacement for the gpt3_api.py file.
       The creation of this new file is the first modification. This code is used to communicate with the
Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct language model via LangChain and Hugging Face’s library. The main function in
this file is make_requests, the same name as the function that communicates with OpenAI’s davinci in
gpt3_api.py. In order to keep the behaviour as close as possible to the original version of SELF-INSTRUCT,
the parameters that influence the responses and the behaviour of the model also follow the example of the
method in the gpt3_api.py file. The reason for keeping as close as possible to the original method is that
it has been tried and tested, and it is not desirable to deviate from the expected behaviour when there is a
change in LLM. This function therefore sends requests to the LLM with specified parameters such as prompts,
the maximum number of tokens, temperature, etc. It manages the attempts, the failure and returns the
responses generated. When a query fails, the function restarts the query with a waiting time that increases
exponentially if the error occurs again and, when the query succeeds, the method collects the model responses
and creates a dictionary containing the data generated. The rest of the file contains the same elements as in
the original SELF-INSTRUCT script. Namely, the parse_args function, which parses command line arguments.

There are several stages in using SELF-INSTRUCT:

  1. Generate instructions: In this first phase, the model must generate new instructions containing new BDD
     scenarios or at least an instruction that will involve a scenario as input. This step uses the collection of
     175 instructions in the seed file to augment the original set with new and different instructions. A few
     changes have been made to the code for this step, which is contained in the bootstrap_instructions.py
     file. Firstly, the method using LLM is now imported from mixtral_api.py rather than gpt3_api.py. Next,
     the filters for recently generated data were adapted. The minimum size has been increased to get at least
     one complete statement, such as “Generate a full Python implementation of the step functions for the
     following Gherkin scenario using the behave BDD testing framework” for example. The maximum size
     has also been increased because the instructions in this context are longer. Finally, the filter concerning
     prohibited words has been completely ignored because in the case of scenario creation, words such as
     “image”, “file”, “go to”, etc. are possible and authorised.

                                                                                                                22
CHAPTER 3. RESEARCH DEVELOPMENT                                                     3.2. THE METHODOLOGY


    2. Determining whether the instruction represents a classification task: This involves determining whether
       the instructions freshly generated in the previous step correspond to classification tasks or not. This step
       influences the different choices to be made in the rest of the process. In the code for this step, only the
       method that calls the LLM was changed to use the new model. The rest don’t need to be changed.

    3. Generate instances using an input-first or output-first approach: Given the recently created set of instruc-
       tions and the results of the previous step, the framework produces input-output instances for them. In
       theory, these input-outputs must correspond and be consistent with their respective instructions. Here,
       the method that calls the LLM was also changed to use the Mistral AI model. It was also necessary
       to change some parameters because the new model did not accept the same values interval. They were
       modified in order to maintain the behaviour originally expected. For example, the temperature parameter
       (the parameter that controls the creativity and diversity of responses generated) was set to the smallest
       value because it needed to remain as low as possible at this stage of the process.

    4. Filtering out poor-quality data: Various heuristics filter out poor-quality or repeated instructions, before
       adding valid tasks to the final dataset. This last step does not involve any significant change except the
       change of maximum size expected, given that the scenarios and their implementation can form a long set.

This process is used iteratively until a great number of tasks are reached, as was done in the study [43], where
the authors reached a set of more than 50,000 generated instructions.

3.2.3     Conducting the experiment
Once the appropriate method has been established and the two seed sets have been created, database
augmentation can be initiated. This crucial step aims to strengthen the database’s ability to comprehensively
represent the various relevant situations or examples. To sum up, for each of the sets that will be increased,
SELF-INSTRUCT start with a seed of 175 examples of BDD pairs and the goal is to increase the set
progressively until reaching 1000 new pairs. The first collection to be subjected to the SELF-INSTRUCT
approach is the one containing the good quality data pairs, called high-quality seed. The process run in four
parts as explained in the section 3.2.1.

Process cycle:
    1. Generate instructions: The procedure is designed to be iterative to ensure that the database is gradually
       enriched. At each iteration, the system is asked to produce 20 new instructions to further refine and enrich
       the available information. This process continues until a set of near 1000 new instructions has been reached.
       Note that the first part of the procedure already sorts the responses of the model. The filters check, in
       particular, that the new task does not begin with an invalid symbol or letter (punctuation marks, for
       example). They check that it is not too short, this avoids instructions that are too short and won’t even
       allow writing the sentence "Generate an implementation of the step functions for the following Gherkin
       scenario". They also check that it is not too long, this avoids some of the scenarios which will not, by
       definition, respect the Essential, Focused, Singular or Integrous criteria. The process starts by using the
       175 examples initially provided to generate the 20 first instructions, and then uses those same 175 data
       and the newly generated instructions in the next iterations. However, in practice, it has been observed
       that the model can sometimes generate one or two additional instructions. These new instructions are
       saved in a dedicated file, which will be used as a reference for the next phase of the process.
    2. Identify classification tasks: The second step involves using the new file containing the generated
       instructions to determine whether the task is about classification. This classification status is used to
       guide the next steps in the process. The results of this part are recorded in another file, which forms the
       database for the third part of the experiment. Here, all the instructions are supposed to be identified as
       not representative of a classification task, as this is not the objective of this work.
    3. Generate instances: This third step takes advantage of the results of the above analysis to define the
       appropriate processing to be applied according to the tasks identified. In this specific configuration, the

                                                                                                                 28
CHAPTER 3. RESEARCH DEVELOPMENT                                                    3.2. THE METHODOLOGY


     preferred processing is input-first, followed by the generation of the outputs needed to enrich the database.
     These instances are also recorded in a separate file, ensuring complete traceability of the entire process. In
     concrete terms, here the method must create an input corresponding to the instruction if it is necessary.
     It will then create the output which responds to the instruction or to the instruction and the input. If the
     instruction already contains the scenario, the input should be empty. If the instruction does not contain a
     scenario, it should at least be created in the input. The output is supposed to contain the corresponding
     Python code.
  4. Filter generated data: Finally, the method uses the instructions and the corresponding instances to
     construct the final dataset. In order to guarantee the quality and representativeness of the data, a filtering
     process is applied to avoid any overlap and create the most diverse set possible. It should be noted that
     during the first iteration, the model was tasked with generating 20 new datasets, but it actually produced
     22. However, at the end of the procedure, the final set contained only 19 elements. These observations
     underline the importance of continuous evaluation and adjustment of the process according to the results
     obtained.

        Fisher’s exact test [13] was used to determine whether a criterion is more frequently met in scenarios
generated from seed scenarios of mixed-quality (control group) or in those generated from seed scenarios of
high-quality (treatment group). We use the standard threshold of p < 0.05 for statistical significance. For the
effect size, we employ the odds ratio. An odds ratio greater than one indicates that the criterion is more frequent
in the treatment group than in the control group, while an odds ratio less than one indicates the opposite. The
null hypothesis for each criterion is that the odds of meeting the criterion are the same in the treatment group
as in the control group.
        There was no statistically significant association for the Essential (p = 0.130), Focused (p = 0.073), Clear
(p = 0.112), Complete (p = 0.067), and Unique (p = 0.151) criteria. Scenarios generated from high-quality seed
scenarios more frequently met the Singular (p = 0.035; OR (95% CI) = [1.03, 2.11]) and Integrous (p < 0.001;
OR (95% CI) = [1.95, 4.01]) criteria, as the entire 95% confidence interval of the odds ratio is above 1. Overall,
the generated scenarios are more likely (p < 0.001; OR (95% CI) = [1.37, 2.81]) to be of high-quality, i.e.,
meeting all criteria. However, they are also more likely to be ill-formed (p = 0.006; OR (95% CI) = [1.32,
6.79]). Moreover, they are less likely to be Ubiquitous (p = 0.019; OR (95% CI) = [0.19, 0.88]) because the
entire 95% confidence interval of the odds ratio falls below 1.

ignoring the Unique criterion would increase this number to around 471, or more than two and a half times the

The difference in the rate of valid responses between the two sets may be linked to the way the final step of
SELF-INSTRUCT judges the quality of the new data compared with the original seed. If there are errors in
the seed, then the filters may judge several bad scenarios to be correct and keep it in the final dataset.
       The results of the sample analyse show that the set that provides the greatest number of valid responses
is also the one that generates the greatest number of unusable elements because they are ill-formed. This is
confirmed by the Fisher’s exact test, which shows that the dataset generated by the high quality seed tends
to contain more high-quality scenarios but also involve a greater number of ill-formed response. In fact, the
high-quality sample contains almost two and a half times as many ill-formed items as the mixed-quality sample.
This observation raises several possible explanations. Firstly, it can suggest that the quality of the input data
used by SELF-INSTRUCT has no significant influence on the response format of the method. On the other
hand, it is also possible to suggest that in the case of high-quality seed, the filters are not yet sufficiently refined
for the BDD context and accept absurd data for our use. Another explanation that could influence the results
obtained is that compliance with the expected format could vary from one LLM to another. The difference
between the proportions of ill-formed in the two samples may also be linked to the fact that the mixed-quality
sample was generated from data of variable quality. In the seed dedicated to this dataset there are scenarios
that are too long, several contain too much detail and others do not respect the Gherkin syntax rules. This
may have influenced the final step of the SELF-INSTRUCT method and allowed more invalid scenarios to take
their place in the final dataset and therefore replace potentially ill-formed scenarios.
       As a reminder, the Integrous, Focused and Singular criteria are the least respected in the mixed-quality
sample. This confirms that the scenarios in this set are either too long, contain noise, do not scrupulously
respect the syntax rules, do several things at once, or explain in detail how things work. In this case, the
scenarios of poor-quality generated by the model were accepted by the filters, which took the mixed-quality
data seed as a reference. This may have led to the acceptance of longer and more confusing scenarios, which
5.2. HIGH VERSUS MIXED-QUALITY SEEDS                                                  CHAPTER 5. DISCUSSION


was not the case for the other set. This one therefore includes more ill-formed examples but also more valid
elements. In summary, while the sample from high-quality data seed provides more valid responses, the sample
from mixed-quality data seed provides a greater number of usable items despite a greater number of invalid
instances. This does not allow us to determine precisely and clearly whether one of the two seeds has a greater or
lesser influence on the overall quality and/or usability of the data. The results only allow us to make hypotheses.

5.2.2     Impact of seed data quality on compliance with criteria
It should also be noted that most of the criteria have similar values in the two samples. Each sample outper-
formed the other on the same number of criteria. This agrees with the Fisher’s exact test. Indeed, the Essential,
Focused, Clear, Complete and Unique criteria showed no statistically significant difference between the scenarios
generated from the different seeds. This last result suggests that the quality of the original dataset does not, in
general, have a significant impact on compliance with the individual criteria. Nevertheless, Fisher’s exact test
showed some significant difference between the seeds for the Singular, Ubiquitous and Integrous criteria. The
difference for this last criterion is also visible in the samples analyses. So, the quality of the seed prove essential
is in the Integrous criterion. This is not only the criterion least validated by the mixed-quality sample, but
also the main reason why a great number of its elements were invalidated in the analysis. Again, the evidence
suggests that having mixed-quality examples within the seed plays a role when the method decides whether an
item is acceptable in the final dataset. In other words, if the examples in the seed respect neither the Gherkin
nor the step rules, then answers that respect neither are likely to be retained in the final dataset. Once again,
one explanation would be that it is the filtering step that allows this type of response to be accepted, as it is
inspired by the seed to know what is good and what is not. There would therefore be opportunities to improve
the results of a future experiment by modifying the last step of SELF-INSTRUCT. If the criteria are met in
the seed, the choice of language model may also be responsible for the variations and have an impact on the
different results.
       Finally, the subsections Qualitative data 4.1 and 4.2 mention the amount of valid data to be considered if
the Unique criterion is not taken into account. We highlight this possibility because sometimes the only flaw in
new data is that its scenario has a similar objective to that of other scenarios. As was shown in the subsection
Qualitative data 4.1, it is possible to find several scenarios which realise and verify a login, but they do it in very
different ways. If we wanted to have a large set of BDD data to use, it would be interesting to keep as many of
these scenarios as possible in order to enrich the dataset. It’s not a question of diversifying the themes, but of
diversifying the ways of doing things and show high-quality examples. Besides, the Unique criterion is originally
intended to be used when writing scenarios in a specific field of application. For example, when creating a real
application, it is essential to respect this criterion. It can be much less relevant and interesting when it comes
to creating a very large number of scenario-implementation pairs intended to be used by another tool such as
Behave nicely. In addition, Behave nicely has duplicates of mixed-quality data in the GitHub repositories it
originally used. Knowing this, it’s better to keep a great number of high-quality duplicates rather than settle
for a smaller number of examples.

Conclusion

This research begins by highlighting the current maintainability and efficiency challenges in software engineering
in our society. Foremost, we explored the field of software engineering and more specifically Agile methodologies.
These are taking an increasingly important place in the world of development. These methods are numerous
and varied, this research focuses on one of them, the Behaviour-Driven Development. The BDD method has the
particularity of using natural language to facilitate communication between developers and project stakeholders.
Although simple to use, this method can involve maintenance costs and can negatively impact productivity.
Therefore, this work also addresses the current landscape of NLP, LLM, and automation processes. This
leads to explore the possibility of adapting a method, SELF-INSTRUCT, which would improve the use of
advanced techniques for automatic generation of BDD tests. The state of the art allows to better understand
the working framework and the limits of existing approaches, as well as to explore the theoretical potential of
SELF-INSTRUCT.
        In the rest of this master’s thesis, we explain in detail the concrete development of the research. The
objective is therefore to adapt the SELF-INSTRUCT method to the BDD context in order to generate a
large dataset of quality scenario-implementation pairs. The approach is broken down into several major steps,
understanding and adapting the method, as well as providing a new seed adapted to the studied domain which
allows the creation of a new large dataset. It is this final set that motivates this experimentation because it
could then be used as a reference dataset for methods such as Behave nicely and facilitate the automation of
BDD tests. The section Adapting the original method 3.2.1 explains how SELF-INSTRUCT was adapted, what
changes were made and why. The Creating the seed dataset 3.2.2 section explains the creation of the two seeds
whose data is of different quality. This makes it possible to analyse the impact of the quality of the seed data
on the quality of the final set created by SELF-INSTRUCT. These sections are followed by the experiment of
the method adapted on the new seeds (see section 3.2.3).
        Looking at the results of the experiment on each seed, we can see that the method was able to generate
a new larger set of BDD data. From the 175 examples, the method generated a thousand new instructions
for each seed. In the case of the seed containing only data deemed to be of high-quality, 45.80% of the data
generated is valid with regard to the eight quality criteria (Essential, Focused, Singular, Clear, Complete,
Unique, Ubiquitous, Integrous). The rest of the data generated for this first seed is made up of 44.76% of
scenarios that invalidate the quality criteria and 9.44% that are unusable because they are ill-formed. In the
case of the seed containing mixed-quality data, 30.07% of the scenario-implementation pairs generated are valid,
66.43% are invalid and 3.5% are ill-formed. This shows that the high-quality seed allowed SELF-INSTRUCT
to generate a greater number of new valid pairs than the mixed-quality seed. However, it also generated more
ill-formed examples in the first case than in the second. In addition, compliance with the various quality criteria
varied from one dataset to another. Each of the final datasets obtained better results than the other in half
of the criteria. Nevertheless, it should be noted that in both cases, the criterion that was least often met was
Unique. In fact, some scenarios were invalidated because they did not meet this single criterion. This concerned
38 scenarios in the first case (high-quality sample) and 25 in the second (mixed-quality sample), i.e. 13.29%
and 8.74% respectively in each set.
        So, the aim of this work was to explore the adaptability of SELF-INSTRUCT to generate a new large
scenario-implementation pairs dataset, to evaluate the quality of the generated scenarios and to assess the
impact of the initial data quality on the results.
        Firstly, the results indicate that SELF-INSTRUCT is adaptable to the BDD context and is able to
augment scenario-implementation pairs datasets efficiently. Indeed, even in the worst case studied here, the
method generated enough valid pairs to at least double the size of the seed. This represents a substantial
increase in the number of good quality pairs available.
        Secondly, with regard to the quality of the scenario-implementation pairs generated and the impact of
                                                                                   CHAPTER 6. CONCLUSION


seed quality on the final result, the results suggest that the quality of the initial data influences the validity of
the response elements generated by SELF-INSTRUCT. Indeed, the data generated from the high-quality seed
produced a greater quantity of new valid pairs than the data generated from the mixed-quality seed. However, in
both cases, the method was still able to produce a large quantity of usable new data. This shows that, although
it is probably sensitive to the quality of the seed data, SELF-INSTRUCT remains capable of generating new
usable and valid data whatever the quality of the seed.
        Finally, the results showed variable portions of ill-formed generated data in both result datasets. In
both cases, this amount can be considered tolerable, as it does not exceed 10% in the worst case seen during
this study. The method’s tendency to create data that is both usable and valid could be linked to the filters
applied during the fourth step of each iteration, which is responsible for accepting new pairs. Indeed, despite
the success of SELF-INSTRUCT in its mission to augment a BDD dataset, it can certainly be further improved
and fine-tuned. Despite the impact of seed data quality on the overall quality of the data generated, it does
not appear to have a significant direct impact on compliance with the quality criteria taken individually, except
for Integrous. This is the criterion with the greatest difference between the two sample sets. In this case too,
the initial data seems to have influenced the acceptance or non-acceptance of scenarios that do not meet this
criterion. A refinement of the fourth step could, once again, improve the quality of the datasets generated. It
should be noted, however, that SELF-INSTRUCT was used with a different LLM from the original LLM, and
depending on the choice of language model, the results and the quality of the results may vary.
        In summary, although SELF-INSTRUCT is a promising method for augmenting BDD datasets and
generating usable scenarios, there is still possibilities of improvement and fine-tuning to ensure the quality of the
data generated. Nevertheless, this research confirms the capability of automated data generation techniques and
allows imagining being able to improve the efficiency and quality of software engineering processes, particularly
in agile environments. Of course, there are still a number of ideas to explore in future work that unfortunately
could not be carried out here. Here are a few examples of topics we would have liked to explore further:
   • Improvement of SELF-INSTRUCT: Redo this experiment by improving the filters responsible for accepting
     the elements generated by the method. In the best-case scenario, this new experiment would give better
     results, and in the worst-case scenario, it would allow comparing the results of different experiments.

   • Larger set creation: This research presents the results of a single experiment with the creation of a
     thousand new scenario-implementation pairs for each seed. However, a larger scale study could provide
     better information to generalise the effectiveness of SELF-INSTRUCT.
   • Using other language models: As mentioned, the choice of language model can have an impact on the
     results of the method. It could be interesting to compare the results obtained using different models, as
     well as to understand whether these models affect the quality and diversity of the scenarios and how.
   • Practical use: It could be interesting to use the valid data generated by SELF-INSTRUCT in tools such as
     Behave nicely to see if this improves the results provided by this tool and provide feedback on its practical
     usefulness.
