\section{Results for Q1: Geographic Diversity of DNS Responses}
\label{sec:4.2}

\textbf{Research question}: What proportion of popular domains return geographically differentiated \ac{DNS} responses, and what infrastructure mechanisms explain the differentiation?

\subsection{Overall Distribution of Geographic Diversity}
\label{subsec:4.2.1}

The geographic evaluation spans two distinct spatial granularities: a macro-scale continental tier (six regional blocks) and a micro-scale national tier (185 sovereign countries). A domain satisfies the geographic diversity criterion at the continent level if the \ac{IP} address sets recorded by probes across two or more continents diverge on any single observation day; the same logic applies at the country level.

Under these criteria, 174 of 250 domains (69.6\%) exhibit geographic diversity at the continent level, and likewise at the country level. This identical detection rate at both granularities indicates that intra-continental diversity is pervasive: virtually every continent-diverse domain also exhibits measurable variation between countries within the same continent, yielding 173 of 250 domains (69.2\%) with confirmed intra-continental differentiation.

On average, domains return 2.65 distinct \ac{IP} address sets when aggregated across broad continental groups, a metric that climbs to 13.7 when evaluated at the national scale. This sharp increase in country-level variance underscores the fine-grained traffic engineering deployed by major \ac{CDN}s, which maintain routing tables that differentiate responses at the individual country level or below.

The 76 remaining domains (30.4\%) returned a consistent set of \ac{IP} addresses across all probe groups. This cohort consists primarily of smaller, single-homed networks that operate without the scale or architectural requirements of \ac{CDN} and anycast frameworks.

\subsection{NSID as an Anycast Indicator}
\label{subsec:4.2.2}

Of the 250 analysed domains, 222 (88.8\%) have at least one measurement result carrying an \ac{NSID} string in the OPT record of the \ac{DNS} response. \ac{NSID} is inserted by authoritative servers to identify the specific anycast instance that answered the query~\cite{RFC5001}; its presence is a strong indicator of anycast-based authoritative \ac{DNS} deployment. The high \ac{NSID} rate (88.8\% at the domain level; 52.5\% at the measurement-result level) confirms that anycast-based routing is the predominant infrastructure mechanism underlying geographic \ac{DNS} differentiation in the Tranco Top 250 corpus.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:4.2.3}

The 69.6\% continent-level geographic diversity rate reflects the edge-heavy architecture of the Tranco Top 250. High-ranking web properties rely on hyperscale infrastructure providers — Cloudflare, Akamai, Fastly, Amazon CloudFront — whose global traffic engineering frameworks resolve queries using regionally tailored \ac{IP} footprints. The jump from a mean of 2.65 distinct \ac{IP} sets at the continent level to 13.7 at the country level indicates that \ac{CDN} operators maintain fine-grained routing tables operating well below continental boundaries.

The 127-day longitudinal depth substantially strengthens geographic diversity detection relative to short-duration snapshots, successfully flattening transient fluctuations and capturing slow-moving \ac{CDN} routing policies such as weekly \ac{IP} pool rotations. The 69.6\% combined rate exceeds the per-campaign rates (60.3\% for Campaign~1, 49.7\% for Campaign~2 on common domains) because the union of observations across two campaigns and 127 days increases the probability that at least one measurement day reveals geographic differentiation; see Section~\ref{subsec:4.1.4} for the detailed inter-campaign analysis.

The near-identical values across all three windows (38.09\%, 38.17\%, 38.18\%) indicate that the dominant change mechanism is the short-\ac{TTL} \ac{CDN} pool rotation that operates continuously, rather than episodic infrastructure events that would produce a step change visible at longer timescales. A (domain, probe) pair that changes responses on consecutive days continues to change at approximately the same rate one week or one month later, suggesting a stable churn process rather than a convergent one. The accumulation of approximately 97 monthly comparison windows places the 38.18\% monthly estimate on a highly robust statistical foundation.

The per-campaign daily change rates are consistent: 38.22\% in Campaign~1 and 37.87\% in Campaign~2, confirming that \ac{DNS} churn is a domain-level property independent of probe selection strategy.

Of the 250 analysed domains, 44 (17.6\%) are classified as fully stable: their \ac{IP} address set does not change across any consecutive measurement pair throughout the 127-day window. The median per-domain change rate is 48.76\%, reflecting the typical behaviour of the non-stable majority: once a domain exhibits churn, it tends to change on roughly half of all daily measurement pairs.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:4.3.2}

The 38.09\% daily change rate dwarfs the 0.6\% daily turnover observed in the Tranco popularity ranking~\cite{LePochat2019}, confirming that \ac{DNS} infrastructure is approximately 63 times more volatile than the domain popularity layer. Top-tier \ac{CDN}s enforce low \ac{TTL} values — frequently between 60 and 300 seconds — to enable real-time load balancing, and because our probes query authoritative servers directly, they observe the raw \ac{IP} pool rotation without caching interference.

The strict alignment among the daily, weekly, and monthly metrics over 97 monthly comparison windows verifies the stationary nature of \ac{CDN} pool rotation throughout the full 127-day measurement period, with no trend drift detectable across four months of continuous measurement.

\section{Results for Q3: RIPE Atlas Geographic Bias Assessment}
\label{sec:4.4}

\textbf{Research question}: Do the documented geographic biases of the RIPE Atlas probe distribution significantly limit the ability to observe \ac{DNS} geographic response variation?

\subsection{Bias in the Observed Probe Distribution}
\label{subsec:4.4.1}

The probe distributions reported in Table~\ref{tab:4.2} illustrate how selection strategy shapes the observational footprint. Campaign~1 (zone-based, 12,838 cumulative probes) concentrates 79.5\% of probe IDs in Europe and North America, confirming the structural RIPE Atlas bias described by \citeauthor{Bajpai2017}~\cite{Bajpai2017}: in a balanced six-continent design each continent would hold approximately 16.7\%, but the actual EU+NA share is nearly five times that target. Campaign~2 (continent-stratified, 150 fixed probes) substantially corrects this imbalance, bringing EU+NA to 20.6\%, at the cost of reduced country coverage (136 vs.\ 179 countries) and a shift from predominantly multi-probe to predominantly single-probe countries.

Across both campaigns combined, data were collected from 185 sovereign countries spanning all six continents: 35 countries contributed a single unique probe, and 150 contributed multiple probes. The 122 single-probe countries in Campaign~2 alone (vs. 33 in Campaign~1) reflect the country-breadth priority of the stratified allocation. Within-country probe density has the most pronounced consequences for Africa and Oceania, where the zone-based campaign averaged only 6.5 and 48.6 probes per country respectively.

\subsection{Statistical Test of Measurement Impact}
\label{subsec:4.4.2}

To determine whether the geographic sampling skew biases \ac{DNS} diversity observations, we apply two Mann-Whitney U tests.

\textbf{Continental bias (EU+NA vs.\ others)}: The distribution of unique \ac{IP} address counts per domain-continent pair is compared between the heavily instrumented Europe and North America regions and all remaining continental blocks. The test yields:
\[
U = 258{,}248.5, \quad p = 0.292
\]
This non-significant result indicates that the EU+NA probe concentration does not produce a statistically distinguishable shift in the \ac{DNS} diversity signal relative to under-represented regions.

\textbf{Country-level representativeness (single-probe vs.\ multi-probe countries)}: A second test compares the 35 single-probe countries against the 150 multi-probe countries to assess whether within-country probe density affects the diversity signal:
\[
U = 134{,}054{,}796.5, \quad p \approx 10^{-13}
\]
This result is highly significant, confirming that countries covered by multiple probes observe a substantially richer \ac{IP}-set diversity than those covered by a single probe. This finding validates the country-first allocation strategy implemented in the second campaign (Section~\ref{subsec:3.3.1}): expanding national coverage yields non-redundant observational data, but a single probe per country is statistically insufficient to fully characterise local \ac{DNS} routing.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:4.4.3}

The non-significant EU+NA vs.\ others result ($p = 0.292$) indicates that the continental-level probe imbalance does not prevent under-represented regions from detecting geographic \ac{DNS} diversity: even a small number of probes from Africa, South America, or Oceania is sufficient to observe that authoritative servers return different \ac{IP} addresses in those regions. This is explained by \ac{CDN} traffic engineering: operators allocate distinct \ac{IP} pools specifically tailored to each region, so a single African probe reliably receives an \ac{IP} footprint segregated from the European pool.

The highly significant single vs.\ multi-probe result ($p \approx 10^{-13}$), however, introduces a critical nuance: while diversity is \emph{detectable} from a single probe, it is not \emph{fully characterisable}. Countries covered by a single probe observe significantly fewer unique \ac{IP} sets than countries covered by multiple probes. This finding empirically validates the country-first allocation strategy: adding probes to under-represented countries is not mere administrative breadth --- it yields genuinely distinct observational data.

\section{Results for Q4: Impact of Resolver Choice}
\label{sec:4.5}

\textbf{Research question}: Do the three major public \ac{DNS} resolvers return consistent \ac{DNS} responses for the same domain queried from the same geographic location, and how large is the divergence?

\subsection{Overview of the Q4 Campaign}
\label{subsec:4.5.1}

The Q4 campaign targeted three public resolver configurations for 233 domains and approximately 200 probes per domain per resolver: Cloudflare (1.1.1.1), Google Public \ac{DNS} (8.8.8.8), and Quad9 (9.9.9.9). Unlike the periodic Q1--Q3 measurements, Q4 was configured as a one-off campaign run primarily in June 2026, with a preliminary test batch in early April (see Section~\ref{subsec:3.3.3}). A fourth condition targeting the probe's local \ac{ISP} resolver was initially planned but abandoned due to API constraints and structural probe-resolver overlap.

The campaign yielded 141,731 valid measurements: 47,346 for Cloudflare, 47,318 for Google, and 47,067 for Quad9, representing approximately 47,000 (domain, probe) pairs per resolver across the 233-domain corpus.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:4.5.4}

The 52--55\% cross-resolver divergence rate establishes that resolver selection is not neutral: for a majority of (domain, probe) combinations, the \ac{IP} address seen by the client depends on which public resolver is used. This is a structural consequence of \ac{CDN} routing: authoritative servers return different \ac{IP} addresses based on the inferred location of the querying resolver, and the three major public resolvers operate from different anycast topologies. Cloudflare's \ac{PoPs} are geographically distinct from Google's, which are distinct from Quad9's, producing systematically different \ac{CDN} edge assignments for the same client vantage point.

This finding complements the Q1 result (69.6\% of domains are geographically diverse from the \emph{same} resolver) by showing that even for a single probe location, the \emph{choice of resolver} introduces comparable response variation. Together, Q1 and Q4 demonstrate that geographic \ac{DNS} diversity operates on two independent axes: the probe's geographic location and the resolver's anycast topology.

\section{Summary of Results}
\label{sec:4.6}

\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{subsec:4.6.1}

\textbf{Q1 --- Geographic diversity}: 174 of 250 Tranco Top-250 domains (69.6\%) return geographically differentiated \ac{DNS} responses at the continent level, with an identical 69.6\% (174 of 250) at the country level, and 173 of 250 (69.2\%) exhibiting intra-continental differentiation. The mean number of distinct \ac{IP} address sets is 2.65 per domain at the continent level and 13.7 at the country level. The 88.8\% domain-level \ac{NSID} presence rate confirms that anycast-based authoritative \ac{DNS} deployment is the predominant infrastructure mechanism.

\textbf{Q2 --- Temporal stability}: The daily \ac{DNS} response change rate is 38.09\%, a metric that remains remarkably consistent at weekly (38.17\%) and monthly (38.18\%) intervals across $\approx$126, $\approx$120, and $\approx$97 comparison windows, respectively. The per-campaign rates are 38.22\% (Campaign~1) and 37.87\% (Campaign~2), confirming that the churn process is independent of probe selection strategy. This temporal alignment verifies that \ac{CDN} pool rotation operates as a stationary churn process over the full 127-day measurement period. Within the 250-domain corpus, a 17.6\% minority exhibits absolute structural stability, while the volatile majority drives the median per-domain change rate to 48.76\%.

\textbf{Q3 --- RIPE Atlas geographic bias}: Campaign~1 concentrates 79.5\% of probe IDs in Europe and North America; Campaign~2 reduces this to 20.6\% through continent-stratified allocation. A Mann-Whitney U test comparing EU+NA against other continents yields $p = 0.292$ (not significant), indicating that the probe concentration does not statistically distort the diversity signal across the combined dataset. However, a second Mann-Whitney U test comparing 35 single-probe countries against 150 multi-probe countries yields $p \approx 10^{-13}$, confirming that within-country probe density significantly affects the richness of the diversity observed. Increasing per-country probe coverage thus yields genuine additional observational information.

\textbf{Q4 --- Resolver impact}: For 52--55\% of (domain, probe) pairs, the three major public resolvers return different \ac{IP} address sets. Cloudflare achieves the lowest latency (median 15.5~ms), followed by Google (24.7~ms) and Quad9 (23.4~ms). The systematic inter-resolver divergence confirms that resolver choice is a significant determinant of which \ac{CDN} edge server a client is directed to, independent of the client's geographic location.

\subsection{Result Limitations}
\label{subsec:4.6.2}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Phase heterogeneity}: The three operational stages (pilot, campaign~1, campaign~2) used different probe counts ($\approx$50, 200, 150) and slightly different domain corpora (100, 248, 201 domains). Analyses covering the full 127-day measurement period combine these conditions. Domain-level results remain consistent because the corpus converges toward the same set of popular domains, but per-domain diversity estimates computed during the pilot phase carry lower statistical power.

  \item \textbf{Platform bias}: Campaign~1's zone-based selection concentrates 79.5\% of probe IDs in Europe and North America, reflecting the structural composition of the RIPE Atlas network. Campaign~2's stratified selection substantially corrects this but introduces predominantly single-probe countries (122 of 136). While the Q3 evaluation confirms that the continental imbalance does not prevent diversity detection, the significant single vs.\ multi-probe result underscores that fine-grained intra-regional analysis remains limited for countries covered by only one probe.

  \item \textbf{Q4 — ISP resolver absent}: The local \ac{ISP} resolver condition planned for Q4 was abandoned for two reasons. First, the RIPE Atlas \ac{API} rejects the combination of an explicit target \ac{IP} and \texttt{use\_probe\_resolver: true}. Second, and more fundamentally, probe inspection revealed that a large fraction of RIPE Atlas probes forward their queries to public resolvers (Cloudflare, Google) rather than to genuine \ac{ISP} infrastructure, making the \ac{ISP} condition structurally indistinguishable from the public resolver conditions already measured. The results therefore characterise divergence \emph{among} the three public resolvers but cannot address whether a true \ac{ISP} resolver would produce responses closer to the authoritative \ac{DNS} signal.

  \item \textbf{IPv4 only}: The experimental scope is bounded to \ac{DNS} \ac{A} records queried over \ac{IPv4} transport, leaving \ac{AAAA} records and \ac{IPv6} routing dynamics outside the dataset.

  \item \textbf{Domain coverage}: The analysis covers the Tranco Top 250 only. Two of the 250 domains were excluded during pre-validation because their authoritative name server \ac{IP} address could not be resolved (see Section~\ref{subsec:3.2.3}).
\end{itemize}
