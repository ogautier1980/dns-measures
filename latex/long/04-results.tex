\subsection{Data Quality}
\label{subsec:4.1.3}

The RCODE distribution of the 7,971 retrieved results is as follows: 97.3\% returned NOERROR (RCODE=0), 0.7\% REFUSED (RCODE=5), 0.5\% SERVFAIL (RCODE=2), and 1.5\% produced no parseable response (RCODE=\texttt{-1} in the pipeline encoding, indicating a missing or malformed \texttt{abuf} field).

The SERVFAIL rate of 0.5\% is lower than the 1--3\% typically reported in large-scale DNS measurement studies \cite{vanRijswijk2016}, likely because the corpus is restricted to Tranco Top 100 domains whose authoritative infrastructure is highly reliable. The REFUSED responses originate from a small number of authoritative servers that block queries from RIPE Atlas probes; these results are excluded from all subsequent analyses.

\section{Results for Q1: Geographic Diversity of DNS Responses}
\label{sec:4.2}

\subsection{Overall Distribution of Geographic Diversity}
\label{subsec:4.2.1}

\textbf{Research question}: What proportion of popular domains return geographically differentiated DNS responses, and what infrastructure mechanisms explain the differentiation?

For each of the 99 measured domains, a domain is classified as \emph{geographically diverse} if the set of IP addresses observed by probes on at least two different continents differ from each other on at least one measurement day. Under this criterion, \textbf{46 of 99 domains (46.5\%)} exhibit geographic diversity across the two-day measurement window.

The 53 remaining domains (53.5\%) returned a consistent set of IP addresses across all continental probe groups. These are predominantly smaller or single-homed services not using CDN or anycast infrastructure.

\textbf{NSID presence as an anycast indicator}: 45.7\% of all valid results carry an NSID string in the OPT record of the DNS response. NSID is inserted by authoritative servers to identify the specific anycast instance responding to a query (RFC 5001); its presence is a strong indicator of anycast-based authoritative DNS deployment. Among the 46 geographically diverse domains, the NSID presence rate is substantially higher than among the 53 uniform domains, consistent with the hypothesis that anycast infrastructure is a primary driver of geographic DNS variation.

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:4.2.2}

The 46.5\% geographic diversity rate among Tranco Top 100 domains is consistent with the findings of previous large-scale DNS measurement studies. Hoang et al.~\cite{Hoang2020} report that approximately 40--50\% of popular domains use geographically differentiated DNS responses, attributing the variation to CDN-based routing and anycast deployment. The high NSID presence rate (45.7\%) suggests that anycast-driven instance selection is at least as significant a mechanism as DNS-based CDN routing for this corpus.

It should be noted that the two-day measurement window may underestimate the true geographic diversity rate: domains with dynamic CDN routing that routes consistently within a 48-hour period may be misclassified as uniform. This limitation is expected to diminish as the campaign extends to weekly and monthly timescales.

\section{Results for Q2: Temporal Stability}
\label{sec:4.3}

\subsection{Aggregate Change Rate}
\label{subsec:4.3.1}

\textbf{Research question}: What is the temporal stability of DNS responses for popular domains?

Based on the two-day measurement window (2--3 April 2026), the DNS response change rate --- the fraction of (domain, probe) pairs for which the observed IP address set changed between the two consecutive daily measurements --- is \textbf{41.1\%}. This figure represents an upper bound on the true daily change rate: a response change observed over a single 24-hour interval may reflect TTL expiration and CDN pool rotation rather than a permanent infrastructure change.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/fig_q2_temporal_stability.png}
\caption{Distribution of per-domain DNS response change rates between 2 and 3 April 2026.}
\label{fig:q2}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:4.3.2}

A daily change rate of 41.1\% is higher than the 0.6\% daily change rate of the Tranco list itself \cite{LePochat2019}, confirming that DNS response content is substantially more volatile than domain popularity rankings. The high rate is consistent with the CDN operational practice of short TTLs (often 60--300 seconds for large CDN providers) that enable rapid traffic redistribution across server pools \cite{Krishnan2012}.

The 41.1\% figure must be interpreted with caution given the two-day window. With only two observation points, it is not possible to distinguish between: (1) sustained daily churn driven by CDN pool rotation; (2) a single infrastructure event (server migration, IP address reallocation) that happened to coincide with the measurement window; or (3) TTL-driven measurement artefacts where two probes querying the same authoritative server within the 86,400-second measurement interval receive responses from different CDN pool rotations.

Reliable temporal stability analysis requires a minimum of 7--14 days of data. Results will be updated as the campaign progresses.

\section{Results for Q3: RIPE Atlas Geographic Bias Assessment}
\label{sec:4.4}

\subsection{Probe Distribution and Geographic Coverage}
\label{subsec:4.4.1}

\textbf{Research question}: Do the geographic biases of the RIPE Atlas probe distribution significantly limit the ability to observe geographic DNS response variation?

The probe distribution reported in Table~\ref{tab:4.2} shows a clear Europe/North America concentration (66.9\% of probe appearances). A Mann-Whitney U test comparing the number of unique IP addresses observed per (domain, continent) pair between the Europe+North America group and all other continents yields:

\[
U = 43{,}827.5, \quad p = 0.000189
\]

The result is statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.01$, indicating that probes in Europe and North America observe a \textbf{significantly different distribution of DNS responses} than probes in Asia-Pacific, Africa, South America, and Oceania. This finding has two possible interpretations: (1) CDN infrastructure genuinely serves different IP addresses to different geographic regions, which the Europe/NA-heavy probe set captures well for those regions but undersamples elsewhere; or (2) the geographic bias of the probe set introduces a systematic blind spot for DNS variation patterns specific to underrepresented regions.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_q3_probe_bias.png}
\caption{Left: number of measurement results per continent. Right: mean number of unique IP addresses observed per (domain, continent) pair, with standard deviation. The significant difference between EU+NA and other regions (Mann-Whitney $p < 0.001$) indicates that geographic probe distribution materially affects observed DNS diversity.}
\label{fig:q3}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Interpretation}
\label{subsec:4.4.2}

The statistically significant difference between EU+NA and other regions confirms the concern raised in the literature \cite{Bajpai2015} that RIPE Atlas geographic bias affects DNS measurement conclusions. The area-based probe selection strategy employed in this study (Section 3.3.1) partially mitigates this bias by explicitly requesting probes from underrepresented regions, but does not eliminate it: Africa, South America, and Oceania together account for only 14.2\% of probe appearances despite the intentional geographic stratification.

A practical implication is that results for Q1 (geographic diversity rate of 46.5\%) represent a conservative lower bound: the true diversity rate, observable with a balanced global probe distribution, is likely higher. Underrepresented regions may return distinct DNS responses that the current probe set cannot detect with statistical reliability.

\section{Summary of Results}
\label{sec:4.6}

\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{subsec:4.6.1}

\textbf{Q1 --- Geographic diversity}: \textbf{46.5\%} of measured Tranco Top 100 domains (46 of 99) return geographically differentiated DNS responses within the two-day measurement window. The NSID presence rate of 45.7\% across all valid results indicates that anycast-based authoritative DNS deployment is the predominant infrastructure mechanism underlying this differentiation.

\textbf{Q2 --- Temporal stability}: The observed daily DNS response change rate is \textbf{41.1\%}, substantially higher than the daily volatility of the Tranco domain popularity ranking (0.6\% \cite{LePochat2019}). This figure is preliminary; reliable stability classification requires a minimum of 7--14 days of consecutive measurements.

\textbf{Q3 --- RIPE Atlas geographic bias}: Probes in Europe and North America observe a statistically significantly different distribution of DNS responses than probes in other regions (Mann-Whitney $U = 43{,}827.5$, $p < 0.001$). This confirms that the geographic concentration of RIPE Atlas infrastructure materially affects observed DNS diversity metrics, and that underrepresented regions contribute distinct DNS observations not captured by a Europe/NA-only probe deployment.

\textbf{Q4 --- Resolver impact}: Results not yet available (Q4 campaign to be repeated).

\subsection{Result Limitations}
\label{subsec:4.6.2}

The results presented in this chapter are subject to the following constraints:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Measurement duration}: Two days of data provide reliable point-in-time estimates for Q1 (geographic diversity) and Q3 (probe bias), but are insufficient for robust temporal stability analysis (Q2) or for detecting weekly and monthly DNS change patterns.
  \item \textbf{Domain corpus size}: Results are based on the Tranco Top 100 domains. Extrapolation to the full Top 10K would require a larger measurement campaign at substantially higher credit cost.
  \item \textbf{Platform bias}: Despite geographic stratification, RIPE Atlas probe coverage remains unequal; the Q3 analysis quantifies but does not eliminate this limitation.
  \item \textbf{Country-level metadata}: Probe country codes are retrieved from the RIPE Atlas Probes API rather than from the measurement results themselves; a small number of probes (fewer than 5\%) could not be resolved to a country code and were excluded from continent-level analyses.
\end{itemize}
