\section{Discussion of Results}
\label{sec:5.1}

The four research questions formulated in Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} guided both the design of the measurement campaign (Chapter~\ref{ch:method}) and the presentation of empirical results (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}). This section interprets those results in light of the existing literature and draws out practical implications for measurement methodology. The campaign spans 50 days (2 April -- 21 May 2026); all quantitative claims are based on the 45-day partial dataset (2 April -- 16 May 2026) and will be finalized upon campaign completion.

\bigskip
\subsection{Interpreting Geographic Diversity (Q1)}
\label{subsec:5.1.1}

\textbf{Principal finding.} 59.9\% of the 247 analysed Tranco Top-250 domains exhibit geographic diversity at the continent level, returning different \ac{IP} address sets to probes in at least two continents on at least one measurement day. At the country level the rate rises to 60.3\%, with a mean of 11.45 distinct \ac{IP} sets per domain across the 173 countries covered --- reflecting fine-grained \ac{CDN} routing that operates below the continental granularity.

\textbf{Comparison with the literature.}

Van Rijswijk-Deij et al.~\cite{vanRijswijk2016} highlighted that OpenINTEL, operating from a single vantage point in the Netherlands, is structurally blind to any geographic differentiation that authoritative servers implement via anycast or \ac{ECS}. Our distributed architecture --- 248 periodic measurements across 173 countries --- directly addresses this gap. The 59.9\% continent-level diversity rate empirically quantifies the fraction of popular domains whose \ac{DNS} responses OpenINTEL cannot fully characterise: a single-location observatory would observe uniform responses for more than half of the Tranco Top-250.

Koch et al.~\cite{Koch2021} showed that the application context determines whether anycast routing introduces operationally significant response variation. The 77.3\% \ac{NSID} presence rate, and the higher \ac{NSID} rate among continent-diverse domains (56.6\%) versus uniform domains (47.3\%), confirms that anycast-based authoritative \ac{DNS} is the primary structural mechanism underlying geographic response differentiation, consistent with Koch et al.'s conclusion that \ac{CDN}-operated anycast infrastructure is the main driver of meaningful geographic \ac{DNS} variation.

Bortzmeyer~\cite{Bortzmeyer2013} observed that probes in different regions consistently reach different anycast instances. The 51.9\% \ac{NSID} presence rate at the result level directly corroborates this: more than half of all \ac{DNS} responses carry an instance identifier, confirming that a different physical server answered the query.

The mean of 11.45 distinct \ac{IP} address sets at the country level versus 2.67 at the continent level indicates that \ac{CDN} routing granularity is substantially finer than continental boundaries, consistent with Li et al.~\cite{Li2025} and Hours et al.~\cite{Hours2016}: major \ac{CDN} operators maintain country-level routing tables that produce distinct \ac{IP} pools even within the same continent.

Xu et al.~\cite{Xu2023} documented that 48.5\% of popular domains rely on the top-10 \ac{DNS} hosting providers. The corpus-level diversity pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:q1b} is consistent with this concentration: geographic \ac{DNS} diversity is largely a property of hyperscale \ac{CDN} operators, not a widespread characteristic of the broader domain population.

\textbf{Practical implications.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{For longitudinal \ac{DNS} observatories:} Any measurement system that aims to characterise the \ac{DNS} responses returned to end users in different geographic regions must operate from multiple vantage points. Even a small number of geographically distributed probes suffices to capture geographic variation currently invisible to single-site measurement.
  \item \textit{For network performance researchers:} Studies using \ac{DNS} records to infer content-server location should account for geographic diversity; for 59.9\% of popular domains, the \ac{IP} address seen from one location does not represent the \ac{IP} address seen from another.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip
\subsection{Interpreting Temporal Stability (Q2)}
\label{subsec:5.1.2}

\textbf{Principal finding.} The aggregate daily \ac{DNS} response change rate is 38.24\%, and the median per-domain change rate is 48.8\%. Of 247 domains, 20.6\% are fully stable; the remainder exhibit substantial daily churn. The weekly (38.29\%) and monthly (38.30\%) rates are statistically indistinguishable from the daily rate, suggesting that \ac{CDN} pool rotation is a stationary process across all three timescales.

\textbf{Comparison with the literature.}

Le Pochat et al.~\cite{LePochat2019} measured a 0.6\% daily change rate in the Tranco list composition itself. Our per-domain \ac{DNS} change rates of 38.24\% are therefore approximately 64 times higher than the list-level churn, confirming that a stable corpus of popular domains does not imply stable \ac{DNS} records for those domains. The \ac{DNS} layer is substantially more volatile than the popularity-ranking layer, consistent with the short \ac{TTL}s (60--300 seconds) that major \ac{CDN} operators configure to enable rapid traffic redistribution.

Van Rijswijk-Deij et al.~\cite{vanRijswijk2016} demonstrated that \ac{DNS} infrastructure evolves on timescales ranging from hours (\ac{TTL}-driven updates) to years (infrastructure migrations). Our 50-day campaign captures only the short end of this spectrum. The near-identity of daily, weekly, and monthly change rates (38.24\%, 38.29\%, 38.30\%) suggests that the dominant mechanism is high-frequency \ac{CDN} pool rotation rather than episodic infrastructure events. With fifteen monthly comparison pairs now available, this conclusion is well-supported.

\textbf{Bimodal distribution.} The histogram in Figure~\ref{fig:q2} reveals a structural split in the corpus: approximately 20.6\% of domains have a change rate near zero, while the remainder cluster toward high-churn values (median 48.8\%). This bimodal pattern corresponds to two distinct infrastructure archetypes: (1) static, non-\ac{CDN} authoritative infrastructure; (2) \ac{CDN}-backed services with dynamically rotated \ac{IP} pools. The bimodal character suggests that \ac{CDN} adoption is a binary structural decision rather than a spectrum.

\textbf{Practical implications.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Optimal measurement frequency:} For the 20.6\% of fully stable domains, weekly snapshots suffice. For the high-churn majority (median 48.8\%), daily measurement is necessary.
  \item \textit{\ac{DNS} archival strategy:} A tiered policy --- daily collection for volatile domains, weekly for stable ones --- would reduce measurement volume while preserving near-complete change coverage.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip
\subsection{Interpreting the Impact of Geographic Bias (Q3)}
\label{subsec:5.1.3}

\textbf{Principal finding.} The actual probe distribution is heavily Europe-concentrated (EU 57.8\%, NA 21.2\% = 79.0\% combined), consistent with the structural composition of the RIPE Atlas platform. Despite this imbalance, underrepresented continents --- Africa (59.5\%), South America (58.3\%), and Oceania (59.5\%), each holding only 2--3\% of probes --- detect geographic \ac{DNS} diversity in a share comparable to or above the Europe detection rate (52.6\%) and Asia-Pacific (47.4\%). The Mann-Whitney U test yields $p = 0.091$, above the $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold and not statistically significant.

\textbf{Comparison with the literature.}

Bajpai et al.~\cite{Bajpai2017} quantified the RIPE Atlas probe distribution and found that 91\% of probes were located in Europe and North America. Our 2026 measurements confirm the persistence of this skew (79.0\% EU+NA), nine years after Bajpai et al.'s analysis. Geographic filtering via system tags (\texttt{system-ipv4-works}, \texttt{system-resolves-a-correctly}) is necessary but not sufficient: the absolute scarcity of RIPE Atlas probes in Africa (218 unique probe IDs across 35 countries; mean 6.2 per country) cannot be overcome by selection alone.

Nosyk et al.~\cite{Nosyk2025} identified geographic bias as the principal limitation of platform-wide \ac{DNS} measurements. Our sampling density analysis corroborates this: European countries contribute 142.7 probe IDs on average versus 6.2 for African countries (Table~\ref{tab:4.2}). However, the finding that underrepresented continents detect comparable per-domain diversity to Europe nuances the pessimistic conclusion that Nosyk et al.\ draw: the geographic imbalance affects within-region statistical reliability but does not appear to prevent diversity detection.

\textbf{Counter-intuitive finding.} The comparable diversity detection rate in underrepresented regions is explained by \ac{CDN} routing architecture: \ac{CDN} operators deliberately serve distinct \ac{IP} pools to Africa, South America, and Oceania because network topology requires it. A probe in Africa that queries Google's authoritative server receives an \ac{IP} pool different from the European pool, so even a single African probe detects continent-level diversity. The Europe-centric RIPE Atlas distribution is therefore not a blind spot for detecting that diversity \emph{exists}, but it does limit the statistical characterisation of regional \ac{DNS} behaviour.

\textbf{Practical implications.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{For Q1 robustness:} The 59.9\% geographic diversity rate is not significantly biased downward by probe imbalance.
  \item \textit{For probe deployment priorities:} Future campaigns should prioritise Africa (6.2 probes per country currently) to enable statistically robust characterisation of intra-regional variation.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip
\subsection{Interpreting Resolver Impact (Q4)}
\label{subsec:5.1.4}

The Q4 campaign reveals that resolver choice is not a neutral variable in \ac{DNS} measurement: querying Google 8.8.8.8, Cloudflare 1.1.1.1, or Quad9 9.9.9.9 from the same probe for the same domain produces completely different \ac{IP} sets in 52--55\% of observations. This divergence rate is consistent across all three resolver pairs, indicating a structural property of the public \ac{DNS} ecosystem rather than an artefact of a single operator's configuration.

Two mechanisms jointly account for this result. The anycast dimension: each of the three resolvers operates an independent global anycast infrastructure. From any given probe, the routing path to 8.8.8.8, 1.1.1.1, and 9.9.9.9 may terminate at geographically distinct resolver instances with different upstream \ac{BGP} contexts. These instances forward queries from different \ac{IP} addresses, which \ac{CDN} authoritative servers use to assign a location-optimised answer pool. The resolver-selection therefore acts as an additional geographic signal layered on top of the probe's actual location.

The \ac{ECS} dimension: Cloudflare propagates the client's full \ac{IP} prefix to authoritative servers; Google truncates to /24; Quad9 suppresses \ac{ECS} entirely~\cite{RFC7871}. Major \ac{CDN} operators such as Akamai, Amazon CloudFront, and Fastly implement \ac{ECS}-aware routing (documented empirically by Wang et al.~\cite{Wang2018}), so they return different geographically optimised \ac{IP} pools to the three resolvers even when the query originates from the same client location.

The practical implication is that the 50\%+ divergence rate observed here is not a pathology but an expected consequence of the \ac{CDN} infrastructure's use of multiple orthogonal geographic signals. From a measurement methodology standpoint, this result validates the Q1--Q3 design choice of querying authoritative servers directly: any study that relies on recursive resolver responses without controlling for the resolver itself will conflate geographic probe variation with resolver-infrastructure variation. The two sources of \ac{IP} diversity --- probe location and resolver selection --- are comparable in magnitude and must be treated as independent experimental dimensions.

\section{Limitations of the Study}
\label{sec:5.2}

A credible empirical contribution requires articulating the conditions under which results hold and the threats to their validity. We identify three categories of limitation: methodological, technical, and interpretive.

\bigskip
\subsection{Methodological Limitations}
\label{subsec:5.2.1}

\textbf{Collection period.}
The 50-day campaign window (2 April -- 21 May 2026) captures only short-term dynamics. Monthly and seasonal patterns --- \ac{CDN} capacity additions before peak traffic periods, long-term infrastructure migrations, or the impact of geopolitical events on \ac{DNS} routing --- are not detectable at this timescale. Van Rijswijk-Deij et al.~\cite{vanRijswijk2016} demonstrated that multi-year longitudinal observation is necessary to characterise infrastructure evolution at scale; the current results should be understood as a temporally bounded snapshot.

\textbf{Domain corpus.}
The Tranco Top-250 represents the most popular websites globally --- the 0.002\% of registered domains that account for a disproportionate fraction of Internet traffic. The long tail of the \ac{DNS} namespace, comprising the remaining approximately 360 million registered domains~\cite{vanRijswijk2016}, likely exhibits different geographic diversity profiles and temporal stability patterns. Results reported here are valid for the popular-domain tier and should not be extrapolated to the broader namespace without further study.

\textbf{Geographic probe bias.}
As quantified in Section~\ref{sec:4.4}, 79.0\% of probes are located in Europe and North America. Despite the three-round stratification protocol, the absolute number of probes in Africa, South America, and Oceania remains small (218, 374, and 350 unique probe IDs respectively), limiting within-region statistical power. Finer-grained analyses (e.g., country-level temporal stability, intra-regional \ac{IP}-set variance) require more probes per country than are currently available in these regions.

\bigskip
\subsection{Technical Limitations}
\label{subsec:5.2.2}

\textbf{RIPE Atlas platform constraints.}
The RIPE Atlas concurrent measurement limit (100 simultaneous one-off measurements) is a hard practical constraint that must be factored into study design. The 248 ongoing periodic measurements for Q1--Q3 leave no room for concurrent one-off campaigns without a platform-level exemption.

\textbf{Probe independence.}
The 248 parallel periodic measurements (one per domain) are each assigned an independent set of probes by the RIPE Atlas scheduler. Probes appearing in multiple measurements are not fully independent: a probe that is offline or experiencing local network issues will produce errors across all domain measurements it contributes to. The 10,862 unique probe IDs across 54,550 probe appearances (average 5 appearances per probe) means that per-probe failures introduce correlated errors across domains, which is not accounted for in the per-domain independence assumption underlying the Mann-Whitney tests.

\textbf{Phase transition in the dataset.}
The pilot phase (2--10 April, 99 domains, 50 probes) and main phase (11 April -- 21 May 2026, 248 domains, 200 probes) have different experimental conditions. Analyses covering the 45-day partial window mix these two conditions. While domain-level analyses are consistent (the main-phase corpus is a superset of the pilot corpus), the probe-count difference (50 vs 200) means that diversity estimates based on pilot-phase days have lower statistical power than those from main-phase days.

\bigskip
\subsection{Interpretive Limitations}
\label{subsec:5.2.3}

\textbf{Mechanism attribution.}
The \ac{NSID} presence rate difference between diverse and uniform domains (56.6\% vs 47.3\%) is consistent with anycast being the dominant mechanism for geographic diversity. However, it does not allow us to disentangle anycast-based routing from \ac{DNS}-level \ac{CDN} routing (GeoDNS) or \ac{ECS}-driven differentiation, since all three mechanisms can produce different \ac{IP} addresses in different regions.

\textbf{Generalisation beyond the Top-250.}
The Tranco Top-250 is disproportionately composed of hyperscale \ac{CDN}-backed services (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, Cloudflare). The 59.9\% continent-level diversity rate likely overestimates the rate that would be observed for a random sample of domains from the full Tranco Top-10K. Readers should interpret our results as characterising the upper tier of the \ac{DNS} hierarchy.

\textbf{Stationarity assumption.}
The interpretation of Q2 (Section~\ref{subsec:5.1.2}) relies on the observed day-week-month rate equality to argue that \ac{CDN} pool rotation is stationary. With fifteen monthly comparison pairs now available this conclusion is robust; longer-cycle patterns would nonetheless require extending the campaign beyond 50 days.

\subsection{Methodological Contributions}
\label{subsec:5.3.2}

\textbf{Reproducible measurement pipeline.}
The complete pipeline --- from probe selection and RIPE Atlas campaign configuration through data collection, Parquet storage, and statistical analysis --- is documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:method} and published as open-source code on GitHub (\url{https://github.com/ogautier-unam/dns-pipeline}). Key artefacts include the probe selection script with three-round geographic stratification, the daily fetch and parsing pipeline, and the Q1--Q4 analysis scripts. This level of methodological transparency responds directly to the reproducibility concerns raised by Le Pochat et al.~\cite{LePochat2019}.

\textbf{Two-level geographic analysis.}
Prior \ac{DNS} measurement studies typically characterise geographic diversity at the continent level. Our two-level analysis (continent + country) reveals that diversity rates are nearly identical at both levels (59.9\% at continent, 60.3\% at country), while \ac{CDN} routing granularity is substantially finer at the country level (mean 11.45 distinct \ac{IP} sets) than at the continent level (mean 2.67 \ac{IP} sets). This two-level methodology is reusable for any future distributed \ac{DNS} measurement study.

\textbf{RIPE Atlas best practices for \ac{DNS} studies.}
Drawing on Bajpai et al.~\cite{Bajpai2017}, Nosyk et al.~\cite{Nosyk2025}, and Boswell and Perkins~\cite{Boswell2024}, we synthesise and operationalise a set of probe selection and campaign design practices: geographic stratification using system tags (\texttt{system-ipv4-works}, \texttt{system-resolves-a-correctly}); direct authoritative-server queries to eliminate recursive-resolver artefacts; credit budget planning for multi-domain longitudinal campaigns; and \ac{NSID}-based anycast instance identification.

\subsection{Data Contribution}
\label{subsec:5.3.3}

\textbf{Ongoing dataset.}
The 50-day measurement campaign (2 April -- 21 May 2026) continues to generate daily Parquet files, with the final dataset to be published upon campaign completion. The 45-day partial dataset (1,564,904 measurements, 10,862 probe IDs, 173 countries) is archived locally and backed up to Google Drive via rclone. The dataset conforms to \ac{FAIR} data principles~\cite{vanRijswijk2016}: stored in open Parquet format, fully documented schema, and paired with reproducible analysis scripts.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:5.4}

\bigskip
\subsection{Immediate Extensions}
\label{subsec:5.4.1}

\textbf{Completing the current campaign.}
The most immediate priority is to allow the 50-day campaign to complete (target: 21 May 2026), then recompute all Q1--Q3 metrics with the full dataset. This will: (i) consolidate the monthly-window stability estimate for Q2 with the maximum number of pairs; (ii) increase the statistical power of Q3 analyses; and (iii) produce a stable, publishable dataset that does not mix pilot and main phases.

\textbf{ISP resolver comparison.}
The original Q4 design included a fourth condition targeting the probe's local \ac{ISP} resolver via RIPE Atlas's \texttt{use\_probe\_resolver} mechanism. This condition was abandoned because (i) the RIPE Atlas API rejects the combination of a target \ac{IP} field and \texttt{use\_probe\_resolver: true}, and (ii) probe-side inspection revealed that a substantial fraction of RIPE Atlas probes resolve \texttt{/etc/resolv.conf} to loopback addresses or to public resolvers, making the \ac{ISP} condition structurally indistinguishable. A future study comparing \ac{ISP} recursive resolvers would require platform-level support for capturing the probe's actual resolver identity, or a custom measurement agent capable of recording the resolver used per query.

\textbf{Temporal extension.}
Extending collection beyond 50 days would enable detection of monthly and seasonal patterns in \ac{CDN} provisioning. Van Rijswijk-Deij et al.~\cite{vanRijswijk2016} demonstrated that multi-year data is necessary to characterise infrastructure evolution at scale; the pipeline is designed for continuous operation and can be extended with minimal additional effort.

\bigskip
\subsection{New Research Questions}
\label{subsec:5.4.2}

\textbf{Q5 --- \ac{IPv6} geographic diversity.}
Do \ac{DNS} \ac{AAAA} responses exhibit the same geographic diversity as \ac{A} responses? The hypothesis, grounded in Bajpai et al.~\cite{Bajpai2017}, is that \ac{IPv6} \ac{CDN} deployment lags \ac{IPv4}, producing lower inter-regional diversity. A dual-stack extension (simultaneous \ac{A} + \ac{AAAA} queries from \ac{IPv6}-enabled probes) would test this hypothesis and inform \ac{IPv6} transition planning.

\textbf{Q6 --- \ac{DNS} security and early anomaly detection.}
Van der Toorn et al.~\cite{vanderToorn2018} demonstrated that longitudinal \ac{DNS} data enables detection of malicious infrastructure changes 100 days before commercial blacklists. Our dataset, which captures both temporal and spatial dimensions of \ac{DNS} responses, could enable analogous early detection based on anomalous changes in the \emph{geographic distribution} of \ac{DNS} responses --- a spatial anomaly signature potentially distinct from purely temporal indicators.

\textbf{Q7 --- \ac{DNS} centralisation and digital sovereignty.}
Xu et al.~\cite{Xu2023} documented that the global \ac{DNS} resolver ecosystem is highly concentrated. Our measurement data on authoritative-side \ac{CDN} concentration provides a complementary perspective: the fraction of popular domains whose \ac{DNS} infrastructure is controlled by non-European providers raises questions of digital sovereignty directly relevant to EU regulation (NIS2 Directive, Data Act). A policy-oriented analysis correlating our per-continent diversity metrics with jurisdictional data would contribute to this regulatory debate.

\bigskip
\subsection{Methodological Improvements}
\label{subsec:5.4.3}

\textbf{Mechanism attribution.}
The current methodology detects \emph{that} geographic diversity exists, but does not attribute it to a specific mechanism (anycast routing, GeoDNS, \ac{ECS}). A dedicated experiment that varies resolver \ac{ECS} behaviour while holding probe location constant --- using a custom resolver that selectively enables or disables \ac{ECS} per query --- would cleanly isolate the \ac{ECS} contribution from anycast-driven variation.

\textbf{Causal analysis of temporal change.}
The observation that daily and weekly change rates are equal is consistent with stationary \ac{CDN} pool rotation, but does not establish the causal mechanism. Hours et al.~\cite{Hours2016} used Bayesian networks to isolate causal effects in a related \ac{DNS} context. A causal inference framework with \ac{TTL}, change rate, \ac{CDN} provider, and domain popularity as variables would determine whether short \ac{TTL} configuration drives frequent record updates, or merely co-occurs with them.

\textbf{Probe diversity augmentation.}
The structural scarcity of RIPE Atlas probes in Africa (6.2 per country on average) limits within-region statistical power. Complementing RIPE Atlas measurements with data from NLNOG Ring, CAIDA Ark, or academic measurement networks in underrepresented regions would enable robust country-level analysis for the geographic regions currently limited to single-probe coverage.

\section{General Conclusion}
\label{sec:5.5}

\subsection{Summary of the Thesis}
\label{subsec:5.5.1}

This thesis addressed the problem of \emph{spatially and temporally distributed \ac{DNS} measurement}: how to design, deploy, and operate a system that captures the geographic diversity and temporal stability of \ac{DNS} responses for the most popular domains on the web, in a manner that is methodologically sound and reproducible.

The motivation was grounded in a well-documented gap: existing large-scale \ac{DNS} observatories~\cite{vanRijswijk2016} operate from single geographic vantage points, making them structurally blind to the geographic differentiation that \ac{CDN} operators~\cite{Koch2021,Hours2016}, \ac{ECS}-aware authoritative servers~\cite{RFC7871,Wang2018}, and \ac{BGP}-anycast routing~\cite{Bortzmeyer2013} deliberately introduce into \ac{DNS} responses. At the same time, the \ac{DNS} resolver ecosystem is increasingly concentrated~\cite{Xu2023}, making resolver choice a confounding variable in any \ac{DNS} measurement study.

Our methodological response combined three design decisions: (i) use RIPE Atlas as the distributed measurement platform, selecting probes across 173 countries using a three-round geographic stratification algorithm; (ii) query authoritative servers directly to eliminate recursive-resolver artefacts in the primary Q1--Q3 campaign; and (iii) design a supplementary four-condition resolver comparison campaign (Q4) to quantify resolver impact independently.

\bigskip
\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{subsec:5.5.2}

\textbf{Q1 --- Geographic diversity:} \textbf{59.9\%} of Tranco Top-250 domains return geographically differentiated \ac{DNS} \ac{A} records at the continent level; 60.3\% at the country level. The mean number of distinct \ac{IP} address sets is 2.67 per domain at the continent level and 11.45 at the country level, confirming that \ac{CDN} routing operates at sub-continental granularity. \ac{NSID} presence (77.3\% of domains; 51.9\% of results) identifies anycast-based authoritative \ac{DNS} as the predominant mechanism. A single-point observatory would observe uniform \ac{DNS} responses for more than half of the most popular domains on the web.

\textbf{Q2 --- Temporal stability:} \textbf{20.6\%} of domains are fully stable over the 50-day measurement campaign. The aggregate daily change rate is \textbf{38.24\%}; the median per-domain rate is \textbf{48.8\%}. Daily, weekly, and monthly rates are all nearly identical, indicating that \ac{CDN} pool rotation is a stationary, high-frequency process. \ac{DNS} response content is approximately 64 times more volatile than the Tranco popularity ranking itself~\cite{LePochat2019}.

\textbf{Q3 --- Probe bias:} The RIPE Atlas geographic bias persists (EU+NA: 79.0\% of probes in 2026), confirming Bajpai et al.'s~\cite{Bajpai2017} findings nine years later. A Mann-Whitney test yields $p = 0.091$, above the $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold. Underrepresented continents --- Africa (59.5\%), South America (58.3\%), and Oceania (59.5\%) --- each detect diversity in more than 58\% of domains despite holding 2--3\% of probes, at or above Europe (52.6\%), demonstrating that zone-based probe assignment preserves diversity observability even with few probes per country.

\textbf{Q4 --- Resolver impact:} The three public resolvers show low error rates (1--2\%) but substantial latency differences (Cloudflare median 15.5\,ms vs.\ 24.7\,ms Google, 23.4\,ms Quad9). Cross-resolver \ac{IP} divergence exceeds 50\% for all three pairs, meaning that the same probe querying the same domain through different public resolvers receives completely different \ac{IP} addresses in more than half of cases, driven by anycast routing differences and divergent \ac{ECS} policies.

\textbf{Overarching answer.} Distributed, authoritative-querying \ac{DNS} measurement from geographically diverse vantage points is both technically feasible with RIPE Atlas and scientifically necessary to characterise the geographic structure of the modern \ac{DNS}. Systems designed assuming geographic \ac{DNS} uniformity will systematically misrepresent the infrastructure underlying more than half of the most visited websites on the Internet.

\bigskip
\subsection{Lessons Learned}
\label{subsec:5.5.3}

\textbf{Platform lessons.}
RIPE Atlas is a powerful distributed measurement platform, but its utility depends on methodological rigour. Key lessons from this campaign: (i) geographic stratification using system tags is necessary but not sufficient to overcome the structural probe scarcity in Africa and South America~\cite{Bajpai2017}; (ii) the 100 concurrent measurement limit for one-off measurements is a hard practical constraint that must be factored into study design.

\textbf{Architectural lessons.}
A pipeline based on daily Parquet files, partitioned by date, scales efficiently for the volumes produced by a continuous \ac{DNS} measurement campaign. The separation between raw JSON storage (temporary, 7-day retention) and processed Parquet (permanent) provides both data provenance and query efficiency. Google Drive rclone synchronisation provides low-cost off-site backup for a home-hosted measurement node.

\textbf{Scientific lessons.}
The assumption that geographic \ac{DNS} diversity can be inferred from a single vantage point is strongly contradicted by our data: 59.9\% of popular domains return different \ac{IP} addresses in different continents. The assumption that probe-distribution bias prevents underrepresented regions from contributing meaningful observations is nuanced by our results: even 2--3\% probe share is sufficient to detect that diversity exists, though not to characterise it statistically at fine granularity.

\bigskip
\subsection{Closing Remarks}
\label{subsec:5.5.4}

The Domain Name System is the invisible substrate on which the Internet's navigability depends. Every web request, email delivery, and API call begins with a \ac{DNS} query --- yet the geographic structure of \ac{DNS} responses is rarely measured from the perspectives of the users who depend on it. This thesis is a contribution toward making that structure \emph{visible}, \emph{measurable}, and \emph{reproducible}.

The 1,564,904 measurements collected in the first 45 days of the 50-day campaign (2 April -- 21 May 2026) constitute a spatiotemporal snapshot of how the Internet's most popular destinations present themselves to users in different parts of the world. They document a \ac{DNS} that is more geographically heterogeneous than single-vantage-point studies suggest (59.9\% of popular domains return different \ac{IP}s in different continents), more temporally volatile than the stability of popularity rankings implies (38.24\% daily change rate), and whose geographic measurement is more resilient to probe imbalance than previously assumed (underrepresented regions detect comparable diversity to Europe with a fraction of the probes).

As Stéphane Bortzmeyer observed~\cite{Bortzmeyer2013}, \ac{DNS} is too often overlooked in studies of Internet resilience and quality of service. With the tools now available --- RIPE Atlas's global probe network, Tranco's manipulation-resistant domain ranking, open Parquet storage, and community-supported analysis infrastructure --- there is no longer a technical barrier to filling this gap. The barrier is methodological care and sustained measurement effort. We hope the dataset, pipeline, and results reported here lower both barriers for the researchers and engineers who come next.
