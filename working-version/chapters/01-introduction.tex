\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:1.1}

The Domain Name System (DNS), specified by Mockapetris~\cite{RFC1034,RFC1035}, is the distributed directory service that translates domain names into IP addresses and supports virtually every networked application on the Internet. Despite this criticality — illustrated by the Facebook DNS outage of October 2021, which made the domain globally unresolvable for nearly six hours~\cite{Xu2023} — the DNS has historically received less systematic measurement attention than other infrastructure layers. The most comprehensive longitudinal DNS measurement system, OpenINTEL~\cite{vanRijswijk2016}, measures over 50\% of the global DNS namespace daily but does so from a single vantage point in the Netherlands, leaving the geographic dimension of DNS responses entirely uncharacterised.

DNS data is also fundamentally ephemeral: every resource record carries a Time To Live (TTL) after which it is discarded~\cite{RFC1034,RFC1035}. Zone administrators can change records at any time, and the DNS infrastructure retains no trace of previous configurations. This ephemerality motivates longitudinal archival: security researchers need historical DNS data to reconstruct the evolution of malicious infrastructure~\cite{vanderToorn2018}; network measurement researchers need longitudinal, geographically distributed data to characterise anycast routing and CDN behaviour~\cite{Bortzmeyer2013,Hours2016}; infrastructure analysts need longitudinal datasets to track provider migrations and DNSSEC deployment trends~\cite{vanRijswijk2016}.

Beyond the temporal dimension, DNS responses exhibit a geographic dimension that single-vantage point measurement cannot observe. Three mechanisms produce this variation: (1) \textbf{Content Delivery Network(CDN)-based routing} — authoritative servers return different IP addresses depending on the inferred location of the querying resolver, directing clients to the nearest edge cluster~\cite{Li2025}~\cite{Hours2016}; (2) \textbf{IP anycast} — the same global IP address is announced from multiple geographically distributed Points Of Presence (PoPs), so queries from different regions reach different physical instances~\cite{Bortzmeyer2013}~\cite{Koch2021}; and (3) \textbf{EDNS Client Subnet (ECS)}~\cite{RFC7871} — the resolver embeds a truncated client IP prefix in the query, enabling the CDN to route by end-client location rather than resolver location~\cite{Wang2018}. %Figure~\ref{fig:geographic_mechanisms} summarises all three mechanisms.

The proportion of popular domains that return geographically differentiated responses, the magnitude and temporal stability of that differentiation, and the mechanisms that drive it remain empirically uncharacterised at population scale. This is the research gap this thesis addresses.

\section{DNS Resolution: a Brief Overview}
\label{sec:1.2}

Understanding the measurement methodology and its results requires familiarity with the DNS resolution process. DNS operates as a hierarchical, distributed database. The namespace is divided into \textit{zones}, each delegated to one or more \textit{authoritative name servers} that hold the ground-truth records for that zone. A \textit{resource record} (RR) associates a domain name with typed data — most commonly an \texttt{A} record mapping a name to an IPv4 address — and carries a TTL that controls how long resolvers may cache the answer.

When an application queries a domain name, the \textit{stub resolver} embedded in the operating system forwards the request to a \textit{recursive resolver} (typically operated by the user's ISP or a public DNS provider such as Google 8.8.8.8 or Cloudflare 1.1.1.1). If the recursive resolver does not hold a cached answer, it performs \textit{iterative resolution}: it queries a root name server, which refers it to the appropriate TLD name server (e.g., \texttt{.com}), which in turn refers it to the authoritative name server for the target domain. The authoritative server returns the definitive answer, which the recursive resolver caches for the duration of the TTL and relays to the stub resolver. Figure~\ref{fig:dns_resolution} illustrates this process.

This architecture has two consequences central to this thesis. First, the recursive resolver acts as a \textit{geographic proxy}: from the authoritative server's perspective, all queries originating from the same recursive resolver appear to come from a single location, regardless of where the end-users actually are. CDN operators exploit this to tailor answers by resolver location. Second, because any resolver can query any authoritative server, it is possible to measure how authoritative servers respond to queries from different geographic vantage points — which is precisely the approach taken in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}
\label{sec:1.3}

This thesis addresses the following main research question:

\textit{How can one design and deploy a distributed DNS measurement system that captures the geographic and temporal diversity of DNS responses for the most popular domains on the web, in a manner that is ethically sound, reproducible, and useful to the research community?}

Four operational sub-questions guide the empirical analysis:

\textbf{Q1 — Geographic diversity}: What proportion of popular domains return different DNS responses depending on query origin, and which mechanisms (CDN routing, anycast, ECS) explain the differentiation? Measuring the full Tranco Top-10K would require on the order of 10,000 concurrent RIPE Atlas measurements, far exceeding typical account limits; this thesis therefore focuses on the Tranco Top-250, which concentrates on the highest-traffic domains where geographic differentiation is most prevalent and the findings most practically relevant.

\textbf{Q2 — Temporal stability}: What is the temporal stability of DNS responses at day, week, and month timescales over the measurement period?

\textbf{Q3 — RIPE Atlas geographic bias}: Do the documented geographic biases of the RIPE Atlas probe distribution~\cite{Bajpai2017} (91\% of probes in Europe/North America) significantly affect the ability to observe DNS geographic variation?

\textbf{Q4 — Resolver impact}: What is the measurable impact of resolver choice (local ISP versus public DNS with ECS) on observed DNS responses, and does it interact with probe location?

To answer these questions, the thesis designs, implements, and operates a measurement system that: (1) measures the Tranco Top 250 domains~\cite{LePochat2019} daily over 60 days — a duration chosen to cover at least two complete monthly cycles, enabling analysis of DNS stability at the day, week, and month timescales required by Q2; (2) collects measurements from $\approx$100 globally distributed RIPE Atlas probes selected via geographic stratification; (3) archives results in Avro/Parquet format following FAIR data principles; and (4) analyses the dataset to produce quantitative answers to Q1–Q4. The measurement design complies with the RIPE Atlas ethical guidelines~\cite{Kisteleki2016}: all queried domains are publicly accessible, measurement frequency respects DNS TTLs, and no politically sensitive content is included.

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:1.4}

\textbf{Chapter 2} reviews the state of the art across five domains: DNS measurement paradigms and the OpenINTEL infrastructure; the RIPE Atlas platform and its biases; anycast routing and NSID-based instance identification; CDN DNS routing and ECS; and domain ranking lists, concluding with a gap analysis.

\textbf{Chapter 3} describes the full measurement methodology: Tranco corpus configuration, RIPE Atlas probe selection and stratification, DNS measurement parameters, data collection pipeline, and Avro/Parquet storage architecture.

\textbf{Chapter 4} presents the empirical results of the measurement campaign: dataset statistics and answers
to Q1–Q4 with quantitative evidence and visualisations.

\textbf{Chapter 5} discusses the results in light of the literature, evaluates study limitations, and draws
conclusions with future research directions.
