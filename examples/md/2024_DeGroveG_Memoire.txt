Problem Statement
   Writing is a critical skill for students to convey their learnings and incorporate them eectively.
However, many nd writing challenging, as I did during this master's thesis. Writing correctly,
clearly, and concisely was not trivial for me. Learning to write is something all students must go
through, and it is also a critical aspect of student assessment.1

  As students struggle with writing, they are increasingly attracted to the capabilities of Large
Language Models (LLMs). The availability of LLM interfaces, such as ChatGPT, allows them to
automate their writing tasks (Liang et al., 2024). A quote from the book  "Make It Stick"
                                                                                       succinctly
encapsulates the challenges we face:
          "...even the most diligent students are often hobbled by two liabilities: a failure to
          know the areas where their learning is weakwhere they need to do more work to bring
          up their knowledgeand a preference for study methods that create a false sense of
          mastery ".
Consequently, teachers and professors are now ooded with generated emails, assignments, and
essays. They face the dual challenge of assessing students' ability to structure their thoughts and
verifying that the students are the true authors of their work, a task that was not a concern
previously.

  Machine learning has advanced in many areas, including authorship attribution, which involves
determining the author of a text. Authorship verication, a technique within this eld, helps
validate the authenticity of a text (Koppel et al., 2008). This is increasingly relevant as LLMs
become more powerful and widespread, making it harder to identify the true origin of written
content (Jawahar et al., 2020).

  The evolution of LLMs introduces more complexity into the development of detection tools
(Huang et al., 2024). As models grow in power and sophistication, it is crucial to assess whether
current methods remain eective and to explore new approaches for developing detection tools.
These tools are essential for ensuring that teachers can condently evaluate the authenticity of
their students' work. This brings us to the central question of this thesis:
              "Is it possible to create a model that can classify text in the classroom setting and
          validate whether it was generated by a large language model?"

2.1 Overview of Authorship Attribution Techniques
  Ever since people rst learned how to write, the need to attribute a piece of text the author has
been paramount. The methods for author identication have evolved signicantly, from a simple
univariate representation of a text (Koppel et al., 2008) to sophisticated techniques employing deep
neural networks and transformers (Huang et al., 2024).
    Author identication tasks can be categorized into three main problems, as outlined by Koppel
et al., 2008;
     The Proling problem: In the absence of a specic candidate, the goal is to infer the
      author's traits, such as gender or ethnicity (e.g. Caucasian or African-American).
     The Needle in the Haystack Problem: This involves a large pool of potential authors
      but only a small amount of text to analyse.
     The Verication Problem: Given a set of candidates and a suspect, the objective is to
      conrm whether the suspect is indeed the author.
    These techniques are diverse and often require solving a multi-class problem. To address these
issues eectively, three key steps are involved:
    1. Feature Extraction: Identifying the relevant features in the text.
    2. Encoding: Representing these features in a form suitable for analysis.
    3. Model Selection: Choosing an appropriate model to solve the specic author identication
       problem.
   Understanding and applying these steps allows for the eective resolution of various author
identication challenges.


2.1.1 The Importance of Features
Before processing any text, it is essential to transform it into a form that a model can understand.
Depending on the type of information we aim to extract from the text, features can be categorized
into two classes:
    1. Content-Related Features: These features provide information about the subject matter
       of the text (Koppel et al., 2008).
    2. Style-Related Features: These features reect the manner in which the text is written,
       capturing elements such as syntax, grammar, and stylistic nuances (Koppel et al., 2008).
   By distinguishing between these two types of features, we can better tailor our models to extract
the desired information from the text.

            Content-Related Features
              These features come directly from the topic the author writes about (Koppel et al., 2008). If an
            author decides to write a piece about a male or a female subject, the whole text will be gendered
            in that way. This will seep in the lexicon used. This will allow for an easier detection of the
            similarities between the two articles (Mana & Sasipraba, 2021). This technique is mainly used for
            problems such as recommendations (Mana & Sasipraba, 2021) but can also be used in authorship
            attribution, with the usage of "Content Words" (Koppel et al., 2008).

              Koppel et al., 2008 describe the use of content-related features as follows:
                  "There are aspects of authorial identity that are not easily captured by the sorts of
                  stylistic features described previously. For example, one author may prefer to use the
                  words start and large whereas another may prefer begin and big (Koppel et al., 2006;
                  Mosteller & Wallace, 1963)" (Koppel et al., 2008)

              However, there are limitations to this approach, which can be addressed using the "unmasking
            technique" (Koppel et al., 2008). This technique has proven eective in the authorship verication
            problem, although it may not always provide a denitive answer.

              Since the publication of Koppel et al.'s paper, new techniques utilizing large language models,
            such as Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) (Uchendu et al., 2023),
            have been developed to address these challenges. These techniques leverage transformers (see
            Section 2.2.2) to detect long-range dependencies in text (Vaswani et al., 2017). Typically, this
            involves ne-tuning a large language model and embedding the text using the same model.

            Text Embeddings

              The rst approach to encoding a text, both in terms of content and style, is to use simple
            embedding techniques. This method allows for the encoding of entire texts and facilitates the
            detection of similarities between dierent pieces of text. It is a common technique employed in
            many natural language processing tasks (Pennington et al., 2014). This approach is both cost-
            eective to implement and oers high accuracy in similarity detection.

              With the advent of embedding models, such as transformers, the capability to encode relation-
            ships between words, even over long distances, has signicantly improved. Techniques such as
            sentiment analysis based on text embeddings (Devlin et al., 2019) have also been developed in
            recent years, leveraging these advancements.

    Complexity Measures: Early eorts aimed to represent text complexity statistically, such
     as by computing word length. Although it may work in specic cases, complexity measures

     words dierently, even when writing on the same topic, making this an eective feature for

           "FWs and some POS features can be subsumed by considering taxonomies, based
           on Systemic Functional Linguistic (Halliday et al., 2014), which represent gram-
           matical and semantic distinctions between classes of FWs at dierent levels of
           abstraction (Matthiessen, 1995))." (Koppel et al., 2008)

     This taxonomy combines both Functional and Lexical features, creating a new classication
     system. It is then used to represent the dierent ways each class of features is utilized.

    N-Grams: N-Grams encode text by capturing the relationship between words in tuples of
     size N. For example, the sentence "Gil is a human" can be decomposed into several N-Grams:

         1-Gram: [(Gil), (is), (a), (human)]
         2-Gram: [(Gil, is), (is, a), (a, human)]
         3-Gram: [(Gil, is, a), (is, a, human)]
     This method captures word collocation and can be used in models to detect patterns that
     are more prevalent in a particular author's work.



2.2 Evolution of Language Models
2.2.1 Early Language Models
   Since the inception of AI, developers have aimed to create models capable of humanlike com-
prehension of text inputs. In 1966, ELIZA was developed to study human-machine interactions
(Weizenbaum, 1966). Using Prolog and its rule-based engine, ELIZA was able to mimic conversa-
tions between a human and a therapist (Weizenbaum, 1966). Although it did not use any machine
learning, ELIZA is considered one of the rst natural language processing systems.


   In the late 1990s, probabilistic models capable of predicting the next word in a sentence emerged
(Jurafsky & Martin, 2000). These developments marked the birth of language models. Since then,
the pursuit of models capable of performing language-based tasks has continued, leading to the
emergence of new techniques based on neural networks (Bengio et al., 1993; De Mulder et al.,
2015). These models have been able to perform various tasks. However, to address some of their
inherent limitations, they grew in size and complexity, making their training time-consuming and
limiting their ability to be used as a single model for all text-based tasks (De Mulder et al., 2015).

            2.2.2 Introduction to Attention Mechanism
              As discussed in the previous section, learning from text can be straightforward using N-Gram
            representations, which allow for the computation of the probability of the next word in a sentence.
            However, the results and implications of such models are limited (Jurafsky & Martin, 2000) and
            depend on the model's ability to understand the sentence's meaning (Niu et al., 2021). To achieve
            good results, certain patterns in the text need to be identied. Humans perform this naturally,
            focusing on context (Niu et al., 2021),but this is more complex for models (Bengio et al., 1993). The
            "long-range dependency" problem refers to the challenge of detecting relationships between words
            in a sentence when the distance between them is signicant. For example, in bigram language
            models, context is lost quickly since these models only capture relationships between adjacent
            words.

               Humans possess an innate ability to understand context and meaning, known as attention.
            This attention can be categorized into two types:

                Unconscious Attention: A bottom-up approach developed as we grow, where we under-
                 stand the importance of words based on external stimuli, such as voice pitch and uctuations.

                Conscious Attention: A top-down approach learned over time, where we grasp the mean-
                 ings and subtleties of words, improving our ability to comprehend sentences in specic con-
                 texts.

               For example, the sentences "the forest is on re" and "the crowd is on re" are structurally
            identical, but their meanings dier signicantly:

                The forest is on re: This phrase conveys urgency, as it describes a literal re in a forest.

                The crowd is on re: This phrase, typically used in a festive context, means that the
                 crowd is highly excited.

               When humans have acquired sucient knowledge of the languages, they are able to discern the
            dierence between the two sentences, unlike children or new language learners. However, neural
            networks do not have this inherent capacity (Bengio et al., 1993). Initially, tasks were performed
            with rule-based machines that processed input token-by-token and applied logical rules to generate
            output (Weizenbaum, 1966). With the advent of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), local
            context could be captured based on surrounding words. However, CNNs struggle with long-term
            dependencies when words are far apart in a sentence (Bengio et al., 1993).

               Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), on the other hand, process inputs sequentially, with each
            input aecting not only the current state but also future states. This sequential processing allows
            RNNs to handle long-range dependencies by retaining information from previously seen words.
            However, this comes at the cost of larger and slower models, leading to increased training times
            and reduced versatility (De Mulder et al., 2015).


            2.2.3 The Rise of Transformers
               Vaswani et al., 2017 introduced a novel solution to these problems through the multi-head
            attention architecture, which employs "self-attention". This approach allows the model to assess
            the relative importance of each word within a sentence and across sequences of sentences. They
            introduced two key methods to compute attention (as illustrated in Figure 2.1):

              1. Scaled Dot-Product Attention: This method computes the attention weights between a
                 query (Q) token and a set of key (K)-value (V) tokens. It utilizes matrices as input and scales
                 the attention scores to maintain gradient stability, even for high-dimensional data. Compared
                 to the previous attention methods, the scaled dot-product attention leverages highly opti-
                 mized matrix multiplication, enabling ecient learning with high-dimensional matrices and
                 thus capturing a broader range of dependencies (Vaswani et al., 2017). This method is de-
                 picted on the left side of Figure 2.1.

2.2.5 The Limits of Textual Learning and Attention
   Transformers-based models, and specically large language models (LLMs), derive their power
from their ability to grasp context from text. This is made possible by training on vast datasets
(Vaswani et al., 2017). They tokenize input text and detect internal relationships among these
tokens. This capability allows them to convince humans that they can reason (Beuls & Van Eecke,
2024; Ippolito et al., 2020). When humans learn about attention, they engage actively or passively
in communication within specic contexts. This enables them to understand the relationship
between their experiences, situations, and the language used at that moment (Beuls & Van Eecke,
2024). Iteratively, they participate in other communication acts, linking new experiences with
2.3.   AI-GENERATED CONTENT                                           CHAPTER 2.       LITERATURE REVIEW



            prior learning. This process renes their initial understanding and helps them identify patterns
            within situations and language, actively facilitating learning.
                To illustrate, consider a parent teaching their toddler about colors using a book. Each night,
            the parent uses the book to describe the relationship between colors and objects by pointing to
            images and using phrases like "the red re truck", "the green apple", and "the yellow jacket". The
            next day, when the toddler sees a red re truck on the street, the parent might point it out and say,
            "Look at this red re truck." The toddler hears the siren sound and associates it with "wheeoo",
            learning that the re truck is red and makes a "wheeoo" sound.
               Later, the toddler encounters an ambulance with a dierent siren sound. Seeing the yellow
            ambulance and hearing its siren, the toddler understands that sirens are associated with emergency
            vehicles, not just re trucks. Through these experiences, the toddler links language to real-life
            observations, expanding their understanding.
               Language learning alternates between "situation-based intention reading" and "syntactico-
            semantic pattern nding" (Beuls & Van Eecke, 2024), as demonstrated in our example. Conversely,
            LLMs are trained using textual content, learning patterns between tokens in large text corpora.
            They engage in "syntactico-semantic pattern nding" without "situation-based intention reading,"
            limiting their comprehension and reasoning abilities (Azaria et al., 2024; Bender et al., 2021; Beuls
            & Van Eecke, 2024; Ippolito et al., 2020).
               Beuls and Van Eecke, 2024, list several inherent limitations of LLMs:
                Hallucinations: Large language models (LLMs) exhibit hallucinations, generating infor-
                 mation that sounds plausible but is incorrect. This issue arises because LLMs always form
                 relationships between tokens, even when the probability is low, resulting in syntactically cor-
                 rect sentences that may lack semantic accuracy (Beuls & Van Eecke, 2024). This problem
                 was particularly evident in earlier versions of LLMs (Azaria et al., 2024; Ippolito et al., 2020).
                Human-like logic and pragmatic reasoning: LLMs contain extensive knowledge but
                 lack the ability to reason about it. Humans can detect implicit context in a sentence and
                 act accordingly. LLMs, however, are trained on input-output patterns without learning the
                 context, limiting their ability to understand why outputs should vary based on context (Beuls
                 & Van Eecke, 2024).
                Data-hungriness: LLMs require vast amounts of textual data to accurately learn token
                 distributions and extract meaning. Training eective LLMs often necessitates hundreds of
                 billions of tokens, depending on the model and architecture (Beuls & Van Eecke, 2024).
                Sensitivity to biases: Biases inherent in society often unintentionally permeate training
                 datasets. To avoid this, developers must curate training data meticulously. Given the scale of
                 datasets required for LLMs, this task is often impractical (Akgun & Greenhow, 2021; Beuls
                 & Van Eecke, 2024).
                In summary, while LLMs have impressive capabilities in understanding and generating text by
            identifying syntactic and semantic patterns, they are inherently limited by their inability to engage
            in situation-based intention reading. This limitation manifests in issues such as hallucinations, lack
            of pragmatic reasoning, data-hungriness, and sensitivity to biases. These challenges highlight the
            ongoing need for advancements in how LLMs are trained and applied. (Beuls & Van Eecke, 2024)

            2.4 Limitations and Challenges in Existing Research
            2.4.1 Current State-of-the-Art Validation Technologies
               Tang et al., 2024 distinguish between two primary approaches to detect content generated by
            large language models (LLMs): the white box and the black-box approaches.
                "The white-box approach is built on the assumption that we control the model and that the
            model, at generation time, can add a watermark that can then be used to detect content generated
            by the LLM. This solution, which is the easiest and most reliable if we control the LLM, is also
            the most prone to fail with the emergence of new open-source models that are not controlled by any
            single entity " (Tang et al., 2024).
               On the other hand, there is the black-box approach. This approach considers the model as an
            uncontrollable entity and aims to detect its output based on patterns inherent in the generated
            content rather than relying on any embedded watermark. While this method does not depend on
            controlling the model, it faces its own set of challenges (Tang et al., 2024).
               Most techniques for validating content generated by LLMs, whether using the white-box or
            black-box approach, rely heavily on curating a dataset specically for this purpose (Shankar et al.,
            2024). However, there is a signicant risk that such datasets could be used to train models to
            evade detection (Tang et al., 2024).


            2.4.2 Challenges in Dierentiating Between Human and Machine-Generated
                  Texts
             As discussed in Section 2.3, most detection tools rely on distinguishing between human and
            machine-generated styles. This task becomes increasingly complex as larger and more advanced
            models produce content that closely mimics human writing (Kumarage & Liu, 2023).

              Nevertheless, distinct styles are emerging from dierent models, even those developed by the
            same company (Kumarage & Liu, 2023). There are concerns with these techniques, such as the
            diculty in detecting specic dierences between models that share the same training datasets
            (Kumarage & Liu, 2023). Additionally, techniques involving style transfer using LLMs (Pan et al.,
            2024) highlights archetypes that models or individuals could use to disguise the use of generated
            content.

5      Discussion
   The results we collected are promising; however, we would like to highlight several points for
discussion. First, we will go through the interpretation of the results from the curation of the
corpora to the model development. Secondly, we will open the discussion on how and why we
believe that our framework could be useful for Authorship Identication, especially applied in a
classroom setup. Finally, we will propose future improvements for this framework, particularly
focusing on dataset gathering and the augmentation step.


5.1 Interpretation of Results
  In this section, we will analyse the results of our research and propose an interpretation of these
results. We will rst analyse the corpora that were crafted and how we believe this impacted the
research. Then, we will analyse the models and their ecacy.

5.1.1 Corpora Analysis
  We started with a diverse dataset, which initially was useful to have dierent classes of texts.
Our dataset included texts ranging from students seeking internship positions to pieces of master
theses. We believe that this created too many classes of dierent texts. As shown in Figure
4.1b, the analysis of the cosine similarity per text type revealed very distinct clusters. This impact
increases the potential for overtting during model training, a potential issue that will be discussed
further in Section 5.1.2.

   Additionally, the dierent classes of texts inuenced the generation step, requiring us to imple-
ment various prompts to eectively dierentiate between these text types. In Section 5.3.5, we
will discuss how we could improve both the summarization and generation steps to circumvent this
limitation in the dataset and help the teacher to have a coherent dataset.

  We also did not explore the use of large language models for embedding techniques to classify
the texts. This will be examined further in Section 5.3.3.

5.1.2 Model Ecacy and Limitations
   As seen in Table 4.2, we achieved very high scores for all but one model. Such high scores
across all models suggest that our texts were easily identiable and that the corpus was too biased,
allowing the models to perform exceptionally well. However, similar validation scores have been
reported in other research (Desaire et al., 2023). These scores should be interpreted with a sceptical
mind.

  It is important to note that our research focused not on designing a new model but on assessing
whether we could use generated text to create a dataset for authorship identication. Thus, we
did not spend much time investigating the validity of these claims. In Section 5.3.4, we will discuss
potential improvements in this area.
5.2.   IMPLICATIONS FOR AI IN AUTHORSHIP IDENTIFICATION                           CHAPTER 5.       DISCUSSION



              Our model implementation was also quite naive. For all the models developed, we used the entire
            content, which limits our models' ability to detect parts of text that were generated. In Section

            5.2 Implications for AI in Authorship Identication
              The overall results of our research indicate that it is indeed possible to use generative models for

            to establish acceptable usage guidelines for large language models.


            5.3 Future Improvement
              In this section, we will discuss improvements that could be applied to this research to address
            the questions raised and enhance the robustness and applicability of our framework.


            5.3.1 Student Corpus Collection in Classroom
               The current research relies on an already curated yet diverse corpus of texts. This diversity
            allowed us to start easily, but we believe it is essential to extend this research by collecting texts
            directly from classrooms. This approach would enable us to gather a baseline for specic class-
            room settings. The collection should be performed in a controlled environment using computers
            to facilitate gathering and processing. Once collected, we could apply the techniques from this
            research to validate their eectiveness on a smaller subset of texts with more controlled diversity.


            5.3.2 Improvement of Models for Hybrid Texts
            The models and the framework we created only rely on detecting based on the full text of a
            student. This approach does not help when student used LLM to generate part of their work.
            Instead of relying fully on the whole text content, we see the benet of encoding the feature on a
            paragraph per paragraph, or sentence by sentence basis. This would also enable us to increase the
            capacity of detection of our texts. This would allow teachers to detect zone of mistrust and use
            this information to ask directed questions to the student.


            5.3.3 Other Embeddings Techniques
              Throughout this research, we exclusively used FlauBERT (Le et al., 2020) as our embedding
            model. While this choice enabled us to achieve high scores with a well-established solution, many
            other large language models also oer embedding capabilities. We aim to validate whether using
            more advanced embedding techniques from newly developed models could enhance the model's
            document classication ability. As these models continue to improve, incorporating them could
            rene our pre-processing steps and ultimately boost the overall performance of our models.


            5.3.4 Model Development
              The models and framework we developed currently rely on analysing the full text of a student's
            work. This approach is limited when students use large language models (LLMs) to generate only
            parts of their work. Rather than depending entirely on the whole text, we recognize the benets of
            encoding features on a paragraph-by-paragraph or sentence-by-sentence basis. This would enhance
            our ability to detect inconsistencies and improve the accuracy of our models. Consequently, teachers
            could identify suspicious sections and use this information to question students more eectively.

  Additionally, using better validation techniques could improve the classiers. As seen in Section

5.3.5 Application of Advanced LLM Techniques
  Our method for generating texts was somewhat naive; we employed a simple few-shot approach
to mimic how a student might use a large language model to generate text. While this approach
proved eective for classifying authors, it may not perform as well when all texts are on the
same subject or require very precise answers. The limitations of our techniques are evident in the
cosine similarity scores between the articles (as described in Section 4.2.3). We found that 230
out of the 482 texts had a cosine similarity score of 1, indicating identical or extremely similar
texts. Additionally, 322 texts had a cosine similarity score higher than 0.5, showing a moderate
to high degree of similarity. However, 166 texts had a cosine similarity score between -0.22 and 0,
indicating low-similarity to decorrelation between the articles. We believe that aiming for cosine
similarity scores predominantly higher than 0.5 would be a good indicator of a correctly generated
corpus. Future research should explore dierent techniques to improve these results, such as prompt
engineering and more sophisticated usage of large language models (LLMs).

  During the course of this research, new models and techniques emerged. We attempted to apply
some of these techniques using LLaMA 3, but due to its late release in our research timeline, we
could not experiment with it thoroughly. We believe that using LLaMA 3 in conjunction with
other advanced techniques, such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) (Lewis et al., 2020),
could generate a more accurate dataset. Additionally, testing the model's capability to generate
prompts rather than summaries as the rst step of our augmentation process could help determine
which generation step works best in this context. Further experimentation with dierent models
or combinations of models can also be conducted to identify the most eective framework for
authorship identication.

   Lastly, with the rapid evolution of models, each step in the research process can quickly become
outdated. When we began this research, GPT-4 was considered the best overall model, but it
was too costly. Subsequently, GPT-4.5 Turbo was released with a focus on cost eciency. On the
open-source front, we started with Mixtral, but soon after, Facebook released LLaMA 3, improving
all benchmarks. Finally, on May 13, 2024, OpenAI released GPT-4o, setting a new state of the art.
This rapid evolution makes it challenging for academic research to keep pace and for frameworks
to adapt. Therefore, we emphasize the importance of validating that the production of a large
language model (Shankar et al., 2024) is accurate and can be trusted without human supervision.
This would enable the research eld to focus on frameworks rather than constantly shifting between
models.

6      Conclusion
  In this study, we aimed to answer the question: "Is it possible to create a model that can classify
text within a classroom setting and validate whether it was generated by a large language model
(LLM)?"

  We focused on using authorship attribution techniques, specically authorship validation, to
build our classier. We encountered challenges in nding suitable datasets for this purpose. Ul-
timately, the primary challenge was not in creating the model itself but in developing an LLM-
generated student dataset. Our main contribution lies in creating a 'twin' student dataset for
training the model.


6.1 Summary of Findings
  In our research, we discovered that while it was relatively easy to generate text using large
language models (LLMs), this process also presented several challenges. Specically, generating
the desired text often required the development of various prompts tailored to specic needs. This
challenge extended to eectively using the generated output.

   We developed several models capable of detecting generated content, as detailed in 4.5. However,
we found that the generated content was dicult to analyse. Techniques such as similarity detection
between articles and extraction of stylometric features proved useful in understanding the nature
of the generated content. This raises the question of why these models performed so well, which
could be attributed to the absence of cross-validation techniques in our initial approach.

  Applying cross-validation techniques to our research, particularly using the dataset of student-
produced texts that were previously rejected, could help ensure that our models are not overtted.
This would provide a more robust validation of our models' performance.

  When examining our model scores, it becomes apparent that overtting might be a concern, de-
spite the fact that our ndings replicate those of other researchers (Desaire et al., 2023). Analysing
these results underscores the importance of understanding how models produce their output. This
can be achieved without resorting to white-box approaches (Tang et al., 2024), making our meth-
ods applicable to all open-source and less controllable models.


6.2 Recommendations for Future Research
  We recommend that future research investigate the capabilities of large language models (LLMs)
to generate high-quality content eectively. This includes developing and testing methods to
validate the generated content across various languages, as the importance of validation increases
with the signicance of the task. To enhance validation, researchers should explore automated
techniques that can be easily implemented for multilingual content.

            and contribute to the development of innovative solutions for text generation and validation.


            6.3 Final Thoughts
               The results presented in Section4.3 provide substantial evidence supporting the hypothesis that
            it is possible to use large language models (LLMs) for authorship identication techniques. The
            high detection scores we obtained, even with naive techniques, were surprising and warrant further
            discussion. One key question arises: "How was it so easy to detect such content?" This opens
            avenues for further research into the characteristics of generated text that make it identiable.

               During interactive testing, we found that while it was possible to fool the model, it also demon-
            strated an unexpected ability to detect generated content in English. This was intriguing because
            we did not anticipate our models to function eectively across multiple languages. Our research
            suggests that iteration on the generation methods and experimenting with techniques such as
            Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) or self-aware RAG could enhance the quality of the gen-
            erated corpus. We hope future research will build upon our work to rene these methods.

               We also observed the impressive capabilities of open-source models like Llama3 and Mixtral in
            generating French content that closely resembled the original text. Tools like Ollama facilitated
            access to these models, and we believe further experimentation with more open-source models could
            optimize performance and cost eectiveness. Our ultimate goal is to develop a framework that
            allows teachers to detect generated content on their local computers, providing this capability at
            no cost. Achieving this would require tting the runtime environment to local hardware constraints.

               We envision future research not only focusing on improving detection methods and also on
            helping students leverage LLMs more eectively. By guiding students to use these tools beyond
            naive text generation, we can help them harness the full potential of LLMs to enhance productivity
            in both academic and everyday tasks.

def cosine_similarity(serie: pd.Series):
    original_embedings = np.array(serie['embedings_original'])
    generated_embedings = np.array(serie['embedings_generated'])
    dot_product = np.dot(original_embedings, generated_embedings)
    magnitude_original = np.linalg.norm(original_embedings)
    magnitude_generated = np.linalg.norm(generated_embedings)

    cosine_similarity = dot_product / (magnitude_original * magnitude_generated)
    serie['cosine_similarity'] = cosine_similarity
    return serie
