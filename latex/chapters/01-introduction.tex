\section{Context and Motivation}
\label{sec:1.1}

The \ac{DNS}, specified by Mockapetris~\cite{RFC1034,RFC1035}, is the distributed directory service that translates domain names into \ac{IP} addresses and supports virtually every networked application on the Internet. Despite this criticality — illustrated by the Facebook \ac{DNS} outage of October 2021, which made the domain globally unresolvable for nearly six hours~\cite{Xu2023} — the \ac{DNS} has historically received less systematic measurement attention than other infrastructure layers. The most comprehensive longitudinal \ac{DNS} measurement system, OpenINTEL~\cite{vanRijswijk2016}, measures over 50\% of the global \ac{DNS} namespace daily but does so from a single vantage point in the Netherlands, leaving the geographic dimension of \ac{DNS} responses entirely uncharacterised.

\ac{DNS} data is also fundamentally ephemeral: every \ac{RR} carries a \ac{TTL} after which it is discarded~\cite{RFC1034,RFC1035}. Zone administrators can change records at any time, and the \ac{DNS} infrastructure retains no trace of previous configurations. This ephemerality motivates longitudinal archival: security researchers need historical \ac{DNS} data to reconstruct the evolution of malicious infrastructure~\cite{vanderToorn2018}; network measurement researchers need longitudinal,
geographically distributed data to characterise anycast routing and \ac{CDN} behaviour~\cite{Bortzmeyer2013,Hours2016}; infrastructure analysts need longitudinal datasets to track provider migrations and \ac{DNSSEC} deployment trends~\cite{vanRijswijk2016}.

Beyond the temporal dimension, \ac{DNS} responses exhibit a geographic dimension that single-vantage point measurement cannot observe. Three mechanisms produce this variation: (1) \textbf{\ac{CDN}-based routing} — authoritative servers return different \ac{IP} addresses depending on the inferred location of the querying resolver, directing clients to the nearest edge cluster~\cite{Li2025,Hours2016}; (2) \textbf{\ac{IP} anycast} — the same global \ac{IP} address is announced from multiple geographically distributed \ac{PoPs}, so queries from different regions reach different physical instances~\cite{Bortzmeyer2013,Koch2021}; and (3) \textbf{\ac{ECS}}~\cite{RFC7871} — the resolver embeds a truncated client \ac{IP} prefix in the query, enabling the \ac{CDN} to route by end-client location rather than resolver location~\cite{Wang2018}. 

While we know that popular domains often tailor their responses based on geography, we still lack a large-scale, empirical understanding of this phenomenon. Specifically, the exact proportion of these domains, how much they vary, and the underlying mechanisms driving this differentiation over time remain unclear. This thesis aims to bridge this specific gap.

\section{DNS Resolution: a Brief Overview}
\label{sec:1.2}

Before any data exchange can occur between a browser and a website, a brief sequence of background queries takes place. This process relies on the \ac{DNS}, which maps user-friendly domain names to the numeric \ac{IP} addresses needed for routing. To achieve this, the \ac{DNS} operates as a decentralized hierarchy of servers worldwide.

Administratively, the global \ac{DNS} namespace is partitioned into distinct 'zones'. Each zone is responsible for specific segments of the naming tree and falls under the management of one or more authoritative name servers holding the definitive canonical records. These \ac{RR}s are strictly typed. For instance, an \ac{A} record maps a hostname to its corresponding 32-bit address. Additionally, metadata like the \ac{TTL} goes with these records, defining exactly how long a cached copy remains valid before expiration.

In practice, a client application never contacts an authoritative servers directly. Instead, a query is first handled by the local operating system's stub resolver, which routes it to a recursive resolver (typically managed by the \ac{ISP} or public providers like Cloudflare and Google). This server checks its own local cache first; if a valid entry exists, it answers the client immediately. Otherwise, the resolver iterates down the \ac{DNS} hierarchy. It queries the root servers first, moves to the appropriate \ac{TLD} infrastructure, and finally reaches the domain's authoritative servers to fetch and cache the record for the duration of its \ac{TTL}. This sequence is mapped out in Figure~\ref{fig:DNS_resolution}.

The consequences of this architecture are central to this thesis. First, the recursive resolver acts as a geographic proxy: from the authoritative server's point of view, all queries originating from the same recursive resolver appear to come from a single location, without taking into account where the end-users are actually located. \ac{CDN} operators exploit this to personalize responses based on the resolver's location. Second, because any resolver can query any authoritative server, it is possible to measure how authoritative servers respond to queries originating from different geographical observation points — which is precisely the approach taken in Chapter~\ref{ch:method}.

\section{Research Questions and Objectives}
\label{sec:1.3}

This thesis addresses the following main research question:

\textit{How can one design and deploy a distributed \ac{DNS} measurement system that captures the geographic and temporal diversity of \ac{DNS} responses for the most popular domains on the web, in a manner that is ethically sound, reproducible, and useful to the research community?}

To structure the empirical analysis, we formulate four operational sub-questions:

\textbf{Q1 — Geographic diversity}: What proportion of popular domains return different \ac{DNS} responses depending on query origin, and which mechanisms (\ac{CDN} routing, anycast, \ac{ECS}) explain the differentiation? 

To analyse the entire Tranco Top-10K, 10.000 simultaneous measurements would be required, far exceeding the limits of a RIPE Atlas account. Therefore this search will be limited to the top 250 domains in the Tranco list (Tranco Top-250), witch are the domains with the most traffic. This is where geographic differentiation is most frequent and therefore most interesting to observe.

\textbf{Q2 — Temporal stability}: What is the temporal stability of \ac{DNS} responses at day, week, and month timescales over the measurement period?

\textbf{Q3 — RIPE Atlas geographic bias}: Do the documented geographic biases of the RIPE Atlas probe distribution~\cite{Bajpai2017} (91\% of probes in Europe/North America) significantly affect the ability to observe \ac{DNS} geographic variation?

\textbf{Q4 — Resolver impact}: Do the three major public DNS resolvers (Google 8.8.8.8, Cloudflare 1.1.1.1, Quad9 9.9.9.9) return consistent \ac{DNS} responses for the same domain queried from the same geographic location, and how large is the divergence?

To address these questions, this research relies on a custom-built measurement system deployed across two successive campaigns for a total of 123 measurement days (2 April -- 7 August 2026). A 9-day pilot phase (100 domains, $\approx$50 probes) validated the end-to-end pipeline, a first 59-day campaign (248 domains, 200 probes) was followed by a second 55-day campaign (201 domains, 150 probes) with continent-stratified probe selection covering 136 countries. This duration covers more than four complete monthly cycles, enabling robust analysis of \ac{DNS} stability at day, week and month level (Q2). 

The methodology incorporates four main stages. First, we monitor the Tranco Top domains~\cite{LePochat2019} on a daily basis. Second, data collection utilizes globally distributed RIPE Atlas probes, sampled via the platform's geographic zone distribution. Third, the resulting dataset is archived in Parquet format, adhering to the \ac{FAIR} data principles. Finally, we process this data to provide quantitative answers to Q1–Q4.

Throughout this process, the experimental design follows the RIPE Atlas ethical guidelines~\cite{Kisteleki2016}. We target only publicly accessible domains, respect \ac{DNS} \ac{TTL}s regarding query frequency, and strictly exclude politically sensitive content.
